\title[AutoM2M: Reliable and Traceable LLM-Assembled Agent Teams]{When the Team Writes Itself: Reliable and Traceable LLM-Assembled Agent Teams through Checked Model Transformations}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Majid} \sur{Babaei}}\email{majid.babaei@ufv.ca}
\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{School of Computing}, \orgname{University of the Fraser Valley}, \orgaddress{\state{BC}, \country{Canada}}}

\abstract{\textbf{Context.} Team builders let a large language model (LLM)
design a team of LLM agents for each task. Such teams often report success when
they have failed, and when they fail, it is hard to find out why.
\textbf{Problem.} Agents in these teams are specified only by prose roles,
every hand-off between agents is free text, and nothing checks before execution
that the parts fit together. We call this the \emph{composition gap}.
\textbf{Approach.} We present \auto{}, which brings model-driven engineering to
team building. A builder LLM proposes a \emph{typed team}, in which every agent
has a metamodel as its interface and every hand-off is a model-to-model
transformation. The transformations run on \tool{}, a runtime we introduce: a
deterministic engine fixes the structure of each hand-off, and LLMs fill in only
individual values, each of which must pass a validator. A checker admits a team
only if six well-formedness conditions hold, including a two-sided anchoring
criterion that requires the goal to reach both the producer and the check of
every value. Trace models reduce failure attribution to a lookup plus a bounded
replay, and every repair must pass the same checker.
\textbf{Evaluation.} We executed 44{,}352 runs on ClassEval and HumanEval+ with
seven open-weight models under eight conditions, including single-agent,
compute-matched single-agent, free-form, critic and shared-schema baselines,
injected 1{,}108 faults, and checked 151{,}516 mutants of LLM-written teams.
\textbf{Results.} \auto{} teams are the most reliable of all conditions: only
5.9\% (ClassEval) and 5.6\% (HumanEval+) of runs report success while failing,
against 18--20\% for a single agent and 27--56\% for multi-agent teams, and more
than 90\% of their failures are flagged as such. Removing the checker doubles
these silent failures. When a team fails, its trace locates the responsible
binding of 96--98\% of injected faults and names the fault class in 74--75\% of
cases, against at most 62\% and 33\% for transcript-based methods; repairing
the diagnosed part matches rebuilding the team with 45\% fewer tokens. The
checker rejects 99.99\% of the mutants in milliseconds. These benefits have a
price: \auto{} uses 7--16 times the output tokens of a single agent, weak
builders often produce no admissible team, and task success is lower than a
single agent's; a pre-registered experiment found that a larger budget does not
close this gap.
\textbf{Conclusion.} Checked interfaces do not make LLM-built teams solve more
tasks, but they make their results trustworthy and their failures traceable.}

\keywords{{LLM-based multi-agent systems, team builders, model-driven engineering, model-to-model transformation, ATL, static analysis, failure attribution, empirical study}}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Software is increasingly produced by \emph{teams} of large language model
(LLM) agents. Each agent is an LLM that runs in a loop with tools and a role,
and the team divides analysis, design, implementation and testing among its
members~\cite{he2025lma,liu2024agents4se}. The first such teams were designed
by hand~\cite{hong2024metagpt,qian2024chatdev,dong2024selfcollab}. A second
generation removes the human designer. \emph{Per-task team builders} read a
task, decide which agents to create, write their role prompts and wire who
talks to whom~\cite{song2024captain,chen2024autoagents,zhang2025metaagent,ke2025maszero};
offline methods search over agentic
workflows~\cite{hu2025adas,zhang2025aflow,zhuge2024gptswarm}; and orchestrators
trained by reinforcement learning pick the next agent, write workflows or emit
whole teams~\cite{dang2025puppeteer,nielsen2026conductor,ke2026masorchestra}.

These teams fail often, and their failures are hard to explain. Cemri et
al.\ annotated more than 1{,}600 traces of seven multi-agent frameworks and
found that system-design issues (44.2\% of failure-mode occurrences) and
inter-agent misalignment (32.3\%) outnumber task-verification problems
(23.5\%)~\cite{cemri2025mast}. Lin et al.\ locate errors on the \emph{edges} of
a team, the hand-offs, where information is dropped, corrupted or
re-interpreted~\cite{lin2026agentask}. Even naming the culprit is hard. On the
Who\&When benchmark of annotated multi-agent failures, the best automated
methods identify the responsible agent in 53.5\% of cases and the decisive step
in 14.2\%~\cite{zhang2025whowhen}. With full input traces, agent accuracy rises
only to about two thirds and step accuracy to about one
third~\cite{chen2026traceelephant}.

\paragraph{The composition gap} We argue that a large share of these failures
has a cause familiar to every software engineer: the parts of the team have no
interfaces. A team builder composes a system out of components (agents) and
connectors (hand-offs). Yet each component is specified only by a
natural-language job description, every hand-off is free text that the next LLM
re-interprets, and nothing checks that the parts fit before the team runs. A
deterministic audit of the 126 automatically assembled teams of Who\&When
(Section~\ref{sec:defects}) illustrates the point: none of the 356 generated
role prompts names a teammate or states what its agent must produce, and no
team plan assigns a step to a named role. Software architecture calls this
situation \emph{architectural mismatch}: components that work alone fail
together because of unchecked assumptions about each
other~\cite{garlan1995mismatch,garlan2009mismatch}. Its classical remedies are
typed interfaces, contracts and specified
connectors~\cite{meyer1992dbc,allen1997connection}. We call the absence of such
interfaces in LLM agent teams the \emph{composition gap}. It gives rise to
\emph{composition defects}, such as parts of the task that no agent owns,
hand-offs whose content the receiver cannot use, artefacts with two writers,
completion that is merely claimed, and work routed to agents without the
needed tools. Today these defects are neither prevented before execution, nor
detected during it, nor attributable after it.

\paragraph{Key insight} Model-driven engineering (MDE) already offers checkable
interfaces for artefacts that are derived from one another. A metamodel states
what an artefact may contain, a model-to-model (M2M) transformation states rule
by rule how one artefact is derived from
another~\cite{czarnecki2006survey,mens2006taxonomy}, and static analysis can
reject an ill-typed or incomplete transformation before it
runs~\cite{cuadrado2017static}. We therefore treat each agent's output as a
model, each hand-off as a transformation, and the team as a network of
transformations. Because an LLM, not a person, now writes these metamodels and
transformations, the composition gap moves up one level: from the messages to
the \emph{team specification}, which must itself be checked.

\paragraph{This paper} We present \auto{}, which applies one principle at two
levels: \emph{LLMs propose; programs decide}. At the level of hand-offs,
\auto{}'s execution layer \tool{} (Agent Hand-Off Transformations) executes
\emph{hybrid} transformations: a deterministic engine fixes which objects exist
and how they are linked, and an LLM fills in only the values that no formula
can compute, each from a declared input and behind a validator. At the level of
the team, an LLM proposes a \emph{typed team}, and a deterministic checker
decides whether the team may run. The checker evaluates six well-formedness
conditions (\W{1}--\W{6}) on the specification alone; each rules out a
composition defect or a part of one. When an admitted team fails, trace models
locate the failed value, a bounded replay classifies the fault, and every
repair must pass the same checker before it takes effect.

\paragraph{What an engineer should get} Our goal is not primarily higher task
success. Two properties matter at least as much when a team's output is used
without a human re-checking every result. The first is \emph{reliability}: when
the team says it is done, the result should be correct, and when it fails, it
should say so. A failure that is reported as a success, a \emph{silent
failure}, is the costly case, because nobody looks at it. The second is
\emph{traceability}: when the team fails, an engineer should be able to find the
responsible part, understand what went wrong and repair that part without
starting over. Both properties are standard expectations of conventional
software, and both are missing from free-form agent teams, whose ``done'' is a
message that an agent chooses to send and whose only record is a chat
transcript.

\auto{} is designed to provide both. Its completion signal is computed by the
engine from executed checks that the checker has shown to cover the goal, and
its traces link every value to the rule, agent and inputs that produced it.
Both rest on the checker, which must catch composition defects before the team
runs. This machinery has a price in tokens, time and, for builders that cannot
write an admissible team, lost work. We therefore ask three research
questions, each about one benefit and its price:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \RQ{1} (Reliability): When an \auto{} team reports that it is done, can
we believe it, and when it fails, does it say so?
\item \RQ{2} (Traceability): When an \auto{} team fails, can the failure be
traced to the responsible part of the team, explained and repaired there?
\item \RQ{3} (Prevention): Does the admission check catch composition defects
before the team runs, and what does it cost?
\end{itemize}
We answer them with 44{,}352 runs of eight conditions on ClassEval and
HumanEval+ with seven open-weight models (RQ1), 1{,}108 injected faults
compared with four transcript-based attribution methods (RQ2), and 151{,}516
mutants of LLM-written teams plus independently seeded defects compared with
LLM critics (RQ3). We then report the price in task success, including a
pre-registered experiment that tested whether a larger budget closes the gap.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}, leftmargin=2.2em, itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{A problem analysis} that grounds five composition defects
(\D{1}--\D{5}) in published failure taxonomies and in an audit of 126
automatically built teams (Section~\ref{sec:defects}).
\item \emph{A formulation} of LLM agent teams as networks of hybrid M2M
transformations, built on standard MDE notions: the semantics of hybrid
hand-offs, the \tool{} runtime that executes them, and typed teams as their
checkable specifications (Section~\ref{sec:approach}).
\item \emph{Six admission conditions}, including a two-sided, feature-level
\emph{anchoring} criterion for coverage; two propositions on their cost and on
the structural coverage they guarantee; and an attribution procedure that
turns failure localisation into a lookup plus a bounded replay
(Section~\ref{sec:approach}).
\item \emph{An empirical evaluation} of reliability, traceability and
prevention against single-agent, compute-matched single-agent, free-form, critic
and shared-schema baselines and two ablations (typed format without checker;
hand-written team), with fault injection, mutation analysis and a pre-registered
budget experiment (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item \emph{A replication package} with the checker, the runtime, the team
fixtures, the analysis pipeline and all raw data (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Summary of findings} \emph{Reliability.} Of the eight conditions,
\auto{} has the fewest silent failures on both benchmarks: 5.9\% of ClassEval
runs and 5.6\% of HumanEval+ runs report success while failing, against 17.6\%
and 20.4\% for a single agent and 27--56\% for multi-agent teams. It flags 93\%
and 91\% of its failures, and its ``done'' is the most informative of all
conditions. The effect holds for six of seven models, and removing the checker
roughly doubles the silent failures. A compute-matched single agent that runs
the public examples before stopping fails silently almost four times as often,
so executing \emph{some} checks is not enough.
\emph{Traceability.} The trace locates the responsible binding of 96--98\% of
injected faults and names the fault class in 74--75\% of cases; the best
transcript-based methods reach 62\% and 33\%. Repairing the diagnosed part is
at least as successful as rebuilding the team (21.5\% against 18.9\% of failed
runs), uses 45\% fewer tokens and keeps 79\% of the accepted work.
\emph{Prevention.} With two-sided anchoring, the checker rejects 99.99\% of
151{,}516 mutants; weaker formulations of coverage admit nearly all teams whose
producers never see the goal. On defects seeded independently by another LLM,
the checker catches 63\% with 13\% false alarms, whereas LLM critics flag
clean and defective teams alike. A check takes milliseconds.
\emph{Price.} \auto{} uses 7--16 times the output tokens of a single agent. For
four of seven models, 45--81\% of builder sessions produce no admissible team,
and mainly for this reason \auto{} succeeds less often than a single agent
(10.6\% against 24.5\% on ClassEval). A post-hoc revision recovers much of this
loss on held-out tasks, but with a weak model it also brings back silent
failures, and a pre-registered experiment found that a larger budget does not
close the gap.

\section{Background and Running Example}\label{sec:background}

\auto{} sits between LLM-based multi-agent systems and model-driven
engineering. We therefore start on the agent side: what an agent team and a
team builder are (Section~\ref{sec:bg-agents}), a running example of a team
that a builder produces (Section~\ref{sec:example}), and the composition
defects such a team can contain (Section~\ref{sec:defects}). We then introduce
the MDE notions on which our remedy rests: models, metamodels and conformance
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-models}), and model-to-model transformations in ATL
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-m2m}).

\subsection{LLM agents, teams and team builders}\label{sec:bg-agents}

An LLM's output is sampled, so the same prompt can yield different answers,
some fluent yet wrong~\cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism,sallou2024breaking}. An
\emph{agent} runs an LLM in a loop with tools until the LLM declares that it is
done~\cite{wu2024autogen,yang2024sweagent}. To say precisely what a team
builder outputs, we need a minimal notion of a team.

\begin{definition}[Agent team, hand-off]\label{def:team}
An agent team is a tuple $(A,\rho,\kappa,E,\mathit{stop})$ of a set of agents
$A$, a role description $\rho(a)$ and a tool set $\kappa(a)$ for each agent
$a\in A$, a set of communication edges $E\subseteq A\times A$, and a
termination rule $\mathit{stop}$. A \emph{hand-off} is the passing of one
agent's output to another agent along an edge in $E$.
\end{definition}

In most frameworks, $\rho$ is free text, every hand-off is a natural-language
message, and $\mathit{stop}$ is either an agent's declaration (for example the
token \code{TERMINATE}) or a turn limit~\cite{wu2024autogen,song2024captain}.
A \emph{team builder} is a procedure, typically an LLM or a learned policy,
that maps a task to an agent team. Builders are optimised by end-to-end scores
or by an LLM's judgement of a transcript; Section~\ref{sec:rw-builders} reviews
them.

\subsection{Composition defects}\label{sec:defects}

Published failure analyses describe \emph{what} goes wrong in multi-agent runs.
To design checks, we need to know \emph{where in the team's specification} a
failure originates. We therefore read the published evidence as five defects
that a team builder can introduce when it composes agents, plus one
consequence.

\begin{definition}[Composition defect]\label{def:defect}
A composition defect is a property of an agent team's \emph{specification}:
which agent owns which work, what is handed over, which tools are where, and
how completion is decided. It is not a property of what one agent produces from
a given input. A specification has a composition defect if even agents that
answer every request correctly cannot make the team deliver its task.
\end{definition}

Table~\ref{tab:defects} lists the five defects (\D{1}--\D{5}) and the
consequence C, together with the published failure modes that each subsumes.
The mapping from failure modes to defects is analytical; we do not estimate
how often each defect decides a failure (Section~\ref{sec:rqs} explains why),
but test whether the checker catches each defect (RQ3).
Single-agent reasoning errors (MAST FM-1.1, 2.2, 2.6) are not composition
defects, because no composition can make an individual LLM reason correctly.

\subsection{Models, metamodels and conformance}\label{sec:bg-models}

Our remedy for the composition gap is to give every agent a precise interface.
MDE provides one: a metamodel, which states what a model may contain, and
conformance, which checks mechanically that a model stays within it. We use
metamodels as the ``forms'' that agents fill in, and conformance as the first
and cheapest check that what an agent produced is admissible.

MDE treats models, not code, as the primary artefacts of development and relies
on programs that check and transform
them~\cite{schmidt2006mde,brambilla2017mdse,naveed2024mde4ml}. A model
\emph{represents} an original for a purpose and keeps only the properties that
serve it; a metamodel is a model of the \emph{modelling language} in which such
models are written~\cite{kuhne2006matters}. A model \emph{conforms to} its
metamodel~\cite{bezivin2005unification}; this is a \emph{linguistic}
instance-of relation, between a model element and the language concept it
instantiates~\cite{kuhne2006matters,atkinson2003foundation}.
Definitions~\ref{def:metamodel} and~\ref{def:model} follow the usual reading of
metamodels as type graphs with constraints and of models as typed attributed
graphs~\cite{ehrig2006fundamentals}. Because each agent will own its own
metamodel, but all of them must refer back to the task, we also allow
references from one metamodel into another.

\begin{definition}[Metamodel]\label{def:metamodel}
A metamodel is a tuple $\MM=(C,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Ref},\preceq,\mathit{mult},\Phi)$
where $C$ is a finite set of classes; $\mathit{Att}$ a set of attributes
$a:c\to\tau$ from a class $c\in C$ to a primitive type $\tau$; $\mathit{Ref}$ a
set of references $r:c\to c'$ between classes, some of which are
\emph{containments} (the target object is owned by the source object);
$\preceq$ a generalisation order on $C$; $\mathit{mult}$ a function that assigns
each attribute and reference a multiplicity $[l..u]$; and $\Phi$ a set of
well-formedness constraints in the Object Constraint Language
(OCL)~\cite{omg2014ocl}. A reference may also be \emph{external},
$r:c\to\MM'.c'$, typed by a class of another metamodel $\MM'$. A \emph{feature}
of $c$ is an attribute or reference whose source is $c$ or a superclass of $c$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Model, conformance]\label{def:model}
A model $M$ over $\MM$ is a finite object graph: a set of objects, each typed by
a class of $C$, with values (slots) for its features. $M$ \emph{conforms to}
$\MM$, written $M\conformsTo\MM$, iff every slot respects its feature's type
and multiplicity, every object has at most one container and containment has
no cycles, every constraint in $\Phi$ holds, and every external reference
points to an object of the right class in the referenced model.
$\Mods{\MM}=\{M\mid M\conformsTo\MM\}$ is the set of models of $\MM$.
\end{definition}

In the running example, the task of Listing~\ref{lst:task} is turned into a
model with one \cls{Task}, three \cls{Method} and three \cls{Example} objects.
Its metamodel, the \emph{goal view}, appears in Listing~\ref{lst:views}
(Section~\ref{sec:app-handoffs}), together with the views of the three agents.
The reference \code{MethodDesign.method : Goal.Method} in the Architect's view
is external: it points from a design object to the goal method it designs.

Conformance fixes what \emph{can} be stated, not whether the statement is
right. We call an agent team ``typed'' in this limited sense: its interfaces are
metamodels, so whether the parts fit together becomes a question about the
features that each part declares, reads and writes (made precise in
Section~\ref{sec:app-check}), rather than about prose. We do not use model
subtyping~\cite{steel2007typing}; every view is used exactly as declared.

\subsection{Model-to-model transformations and ATL}\label{sec:bg-m2m}

A hand-off between agents derives one artefact from others, for example a
design from requirements or code from a design. MDE describes such derivations
as model transformations, ``the heart and soul'' of
MDE~\cite{sendall2003heart}. A model transformation automatically produces a
model from one or more source models~\cite{hoppner2022mtl,czarnecki2006survey}.
It is \emph{exogenous} if source and target metamodels
differ~\cite{mens2006taxonomy}, and its \emph{intent} fixes the properties it
must satisfy, such as completeness~\cite{lucio2016intents}. In \auto{}, a
hand-off may read the outputs of several agents but writes into one agent's
view, which motivates the following restricted form.

\begin{definition}[M2M transformation]\label{def:m2m}
An M2M transformation is a relation
$T\subseteq\Mods{\MM_{s_1}}\times\dots\times\Mods{\MM_{s_k}}\times\Mods{\MM_t}$
between models of $k\ge1$ source metamodels and models of one target metamodel,
specified by a set of rules. It is \emph{functional} if every tuple of sources
is related to exactly one target.
\end{definition}

A hand-off in a software team is therefore an exogenous, unidirectional
transformation whose intent includes completeness: everything the sources state
must be reflected somewhere in the target. In the running example, the
Developer's hand-off \code{Design2Impl} reads the Architect's and the Tester's
views and writes the Developer's view.

We build on the rule language and execution model of
ATL~\cite{jouault2008atl,jouault2006transforming}, a transformation language
that combines declarative and imperative constructs, restricted to its
declarative core. ATL transformations are functional. The basic unit of an ATL
transformation is the matched rule.

\begin{definition}[Matched rule]\label{def:rule}
A matched rule is a tuple $r=(p_r,g_r,t_r,B_r)$ where the \emph{source pattern}
$p_r$ is a list of typed variables $x_i:C_i$ over source classes, the
\emph{guard} $g_r$ is a Boolean OCL expression over them, the \emph{target
pattern} $t_r$ is a list of typed variables over target classes, and the
\emph{bindings} $B_r$ are assignments $f\leftarrow e$ of an OCL expression $e$ to
a feature $f$ of a target variable. A \emph{match} of $r$ is an assignment of
source objects to $p_r$ that satisfies $g_r$.
\end{definition}

In the running example, the Tester's rule \code{Method2Test}
(Listing~\ref{lst:rules}) has the source pattern \code{m : Goal!Method}, no
guard, the target pattern \code{t : Test!TestCase}, and among its bindings
\code{name <- m.name}. Each goal method is a match, so the rule creates one
test case per method.

ATL executes matched rules in two phases~\cite{jouault2008atl}. The
\emph{matching} phase computes every match, allocates its target objects and
records a trace link. The \emph{initialisation} phase evaluates the bindings.
When a reference binding yields a source object, ATL \emph{resolves} it: the
target receives the object that was created from that source object, not the
source object itself. Sources are read-only and targets are write-only. ATL
also requires, and checks at run time, that each tuple of source objects is
matched by at most one rule, so the result does not depend on rule order.
Under this assumption, the target's \emph{structure} is a deterministic
function of the sources, and every target object is accounted for by a trace
link. Many errors, such as ill-typed navigation, unresolved bindings, rule
conflicts and missing mandatory features, can then be found
statically~\cite{cuadrado2017static}.

\begin{definition}[Trace link and trace model]\label{def:trace}
Let $\mathcal{R}_T$ be the rules of a transformation $T$ and
$\mathit{Match}_T$ the set of their matches in one execution. A trace link is a
triple $(m,t,r)$ of a match $m$, the set $t$ of target objects created for it,
and the rule $r\in\mathcal{R}_T$. The \emph{trace model}
$\TL_T\subseteq\mathit{Match}_T\times 2^{\mathit{Obj}_t}\times\mathcal{R}_T$ is
the set of trace links of that execution, persisted as a model in its own
right~\cite{jouault2005traceability,vara2014metagem}.
\end{definition}

ATL's trace links can be exported as models~\cite{jouault2005traceability} or
generated as first-class, queryable models~\cite{vara2014metagem}, and
incremental execution re-runs only the rules whose matches
changed~\cite{jouault2010incremental}. \auto{} relies on both: traces locate
failures, and incrementality lets the runtime recompute only values whose
inputs changed.

Three further notions describe \auto{} as a whole. A \emph{megamodel} is a
model whose elements are models, metamodels and
transformations~\cite{bezivin2005unification}; a typed team is a megamodel. A
\emph{higher-order transformation} is one whose source or target is itself a
transformation model~\cite{tisi2009hot}; \auto{}'s compiler, which generates
executable rules from a typed team, is one. Finally, when a megamodel is kept
causally connected to a running system, so that changing the model changes the
system (models@run.time~\cite{blair2009runtime}), transforming it is a way to
repair the system; \auto{}'s repairs work in this way. To avoid a confusion of
terms: the name \tool{} abbreviates \emph{Agent Hand-Off Transformations}; only
its compiler is a higher-order transformation in the sense above.

\section{Approach}\label{sec:approach}

\auto{} applies one idea at two levels. At the level of hand-offs, its
execution layer \tool{} lets an LLM write individual \emph{values} while a
deterministic engine owns the \emph{structure} of each hand-off. At the level of
the team, \auto{} lets an LLM \emph{propose} a team while a deterministic
checker owns the decision to \emph{admit} it. Section~\ref{sec:app-overview}
gives an overview. The following sections introduce the parts in the order in
which each builds on the previous one: hybrid hand-offs, the language in which
teams are written (Section~\ref{sec:app-handoffs}); typed teams, which a builder
proposes (Section~\ref{sec:app-typed}); how an admitted team runs
(Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}); the admission check
(Section~\ref{sec:app-check}); how a builder uses that check
(Section~\ref{sec:app-build}); and how failures are attributed and repaired
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}). Implementation details are reported with the
evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the eight components of \auto{}. The compiler
and the runtime (\ding{176}, \ding{177}) form \tool{}, which executes a typed
team. The other components build, check, diagnose and repair the team. As in
any model-driven tool chain~\cite{bezivin2005unification,brambilla2017mdse},
the artefacts that flow between components are \emph{models} that conform to
metamodels (the goal model, the typed team, trace models and fault reports) and
\emph{transformations} (hand-offs and the rule modules compiled from them).
The compiler is a higher-order transformation~\cite{tisi2009hot}, since it
produces transformations, and so is a repair delta whenever it changes
hand-offs.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{172} Lifter.} Parses the task text into a \emph{goal
model}, which records the task's classes, methods and examples. This is a
deterministic text-to-model \emph{injection}, that is, parsing text into a
model~\cite{brambilla2017mdse,bruneliere2014modisco}.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{173} Builder.} The only component that designs. Its
\emph{Proposer} samples several candidate teams, each constrained to the
typed-team format (step~A), and the checker ranks them (step~B). Its
\emph{Reviser} then rewrites only those parts of the best candidate that the
checker's diagnostics name (step~C; Section~\ref{sec:app-build}).

\noindent\textbf{\ding{174} Typed team.} The builder's output is a model, not a
set of role prompts. It states the agents and their views, the hand-offs
between views, who may write what, which tools each agent has, what must be
covered and what is delivered, and when the run is done
(Section~\ref{sec:app-typed}). Because it is a model, a program can analyse it
before it runs~\cite{cuadrado2017static}.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{175} Checker.} Decides whether a team is admitted by six
conditions, \W{1}--\W{6}, which cover typing, single writers, completeness,
coverage, completion and tools (step~B; Section~\ref{sec:app-check}). Its
diagnostics name the offending element, and their number ranks the builder's
candidates. A team that is still rejected after $k_{\mathit{adm}}$ revision
rounds is refused (\textsc{NotAdmitted}).

\noindent\textbf{\ding{176} Compiler.} Generates executable metamodels and rule
modules from an admitted team and loads the goal model (step~D). In MDE terms it
is a \emph{synthesis} higher-order transformation.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{177} Runtime.} Executes the hand-offs
(Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}). The engine creates all objects and links. For
each value that no formula can compute, it asks the LLM of the agent that owns
the target view, and a validator accepts or rejects the value. Agents never
converse with each other. The engine, not an agent, decides when the run is
done.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{178} Attribution.} When the run is not done (step~E),
the locator follows trace links to the value that failed, and a bounded replay
of that value decides why it failed (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}).

\noindent\textbf{\ding{179} Repair.} Turns a fault report into a \emph{delta},
an edit of the team model~\cite{kehrer2011lifting,tinnes2025evolution}.
Faults that need no redesign (for example a retry or a wider input) are
repaired mechanically; other deltas are proposed by the builder's LLM. Every
delta takes effect only after the checker admits the edited team (step~F).

\auto{} invents no new modelling concepts. It reinterprets an agent team in
established MDE terms and asks which classical guarantees survive when LLMs
write the transformations and produce their values. Table~\ref{tab:mapping}
summarises this correspondence. Two findings from the MDE literature shaped the
design. First, developers and experts rank \emph{traceability} among the
transformation-language capabilities that matter
most~\cite{hoppner2024traceability,hoppner2022mtl}; \auto{} keeps traces as
first-class models and uses them not only for change propagation but also for
attribution. Second, model evolution is described by edit operations whose
effect can be expressed at the level of the modeller's intent and traced across
metamodel and model
layers~\cite{kehrer2011lifting,tinnes2025evolution,homolka2026coevolution};
\auto{}'s repair deltas are such operations on the team model.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:auto} summarises the flow as a loop. \textsc{RunTeam}
includes compilation, and \textsc{Build} is detailed in
Algorithm~\ref{alg:build} (Section~\ref{sec:app-build}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\auto{}: build, admit, run, attribute, repair}\label{alg:auto}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require task $x$; budgets $k_{\mathit{prop}}$, $k_{\mathit{rev}}$, $k_{\mathit{adm}}$ (building), $k_{\mathit{rep}}$ (repair rounds)
\State $M_0\gets\textsc{Lift}(x)$;\ \ $(\Team,\mathit{diag})\gets\textsc{Build}(x,M_0;k_{\mathit{prop}},k_{\mathit{rev}},k_{\mathit{adm}})$ \Comment{Steps A--C, Alg.~\ref{alg:build}}
\If{$\mathit{diag}\neq\emptyset$} \Return \textsc{NotAdmitted}$(\mathit{diag})$ \EndIf
\For{$j=0$ \textbf{to} $k_{\mathit{rep}}$}
  \State $(\mathit{ok},F)\gets\textsc{RunTeam}(\Team,M_0)$ \Comment{Step D; $\mathit{ok}$: $\varphi$ holds; $F$: failed bindings}
  \If{$\mathit{ok}$} \Return \textsc{Done}(deliverables) \EndIf
  \If{$j<k_{\mathit{rep}}$}
    \State $\Delta\gets\textsc{Repair}(\Team,\{\textsc{Attribute}(f)\mid f\in F\})$ \Comment{Step E, Alg.~\ref{alg:attr}}
    \State \textbf{if} $\textsc{Admit}(\Team\oplus\Delta)=\emptyset$ \textbf{then} $\Team\gets\textsc{Apply}(\Team,\Delta)$ \Comment{F; else $\Team$ unchanged}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return \textsc{Failed}(fault reports)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{The running example through Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}} The lifter
turns \code{TaskBoard} into a goal model with one task, three methods and three
examples. The builder's first proposal for \devteam{} has an Architect, a
Tester and a Developer, but contains one instance of each defect; the checker
rejects it with five diagnostics, and the reviser repairs the named parts until
the team is admitted (Section~\ref{sec:app-check}). The compiled team then runs.
The implementation of \code{mark\_done} fails, attribution traces the failure to
the Tester's input, and a mechanical repair widens that input, after which the
engine declares the run done (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}).

\subsection{Hybrid hand-offs}\label{sec:app-handoffs}

This section defines the language in which every hand-off of a typed team is
written: views as the agents' interfaces, and hybrid rules that split each
hand-off between a deterministic engine and an LLM.

\paragraph{Views} Every agent owns a \emph{view}, a metamodel
(Definition~\ref{def:metamodel}) that fixes what the agent may state. Views stay
heterogeneous: each is sized to its agent's job, and how views correspond is
recorded by trace models (Definition~\ref{def:trace}) rather than by a shared
schema, as in view-based
development~\cite{klare2021vitruvius,bruneliere2019views}. Metamodels of this
kind are also used to define the views through which information about a system
is retrieved~\cite{kirschner2025retriever} and the inputs of model-based
analyses~\cite{cortellessa2022performance}. One view is special: the
\emph{goal view} $\MM_0$, into which the lifter turns the task. The lifter
separates examples from prose: every docstring example becomes an object of its
own rather than a part of the docstring text. Whether a hand-off reads the
examples is therefore visible in its data flow, which the coverage check of
Section~\ref{sec:app-check} relies on.

\begin{lstlisting}[float=tb, language=KM3, style=model, caption={Goal view and agent views of the typed \devteam{} in KM3 notation~\cite{jouault2006km3}; \code{Goal.Method} is an external (cross-view) reference.}, label={lst:views}]
package Goal {                                                   -- MM0, filled by Lift
  class Task       { attribute name : String;  attribute description : String;
                     reference methods [1-*] container : Method;
                     reference examples [0-*] : Example; }       -- in docstring order
  class Method     { attribute name : String;  attribute signature : String;
                     attribute docstring : String;  reference task : Task;
                     reference examples [0-*] container : Example; }
  class Example    { attribute call : String;  attribute expected : String; } }
package Design { class MethodDesign { attribute name : String;  attribute contract : String;
                                      reference method : Goal.Method; } }
package Test   { class TestCase     { attribute name : String;  attribute code : String;
                                      reference method : Goal.Method; } }
package Code   { class MethodImpl   { attribute name : String;  attribute body : String;
                                      reference method : Goal.Method; } }
\end{lstlisting}

In the running example, the lifter parses the class skeleton of
Listing~\ref{lst:task} into a goal model $M_0$ with one \cls{Task}, three
\cls{Method}s and three \cls{Example}s (Listing~\ref{lst:views}). Each method
contains its own examples; the task additionally lists all examples in
docstring order, which later lets a hand-off read the examples of other methods.
A rule that reads only \code{Method.docstring} does not see the examples.

\paragraph{Hybrid rules} A hand-off is written as a set of matched rules
(Definition~\ref{def:rule}). Some of its bindings can be computed by a formula,
such as copying a method's name; others, such as the body of a method, can only
be written by an LLM. \tool{} splits every rule along the line that ATL's
two-phase execution already draws: matching, object creation and reference
resolution stay with a deterministic engine, and only the bindings whose value
no OCL expression can compute are delegated to an LLM.

\begin{definition}[Hybrid rule, stochastic binding]\label{def:hybrid}
A hybrid rule is a matched rule whose bindings are partitioned as
$B_r=B_r^{\mathrm{str}}\uplus B_r^{\mathrm{sto}}$. A \emph{structural} binding
$f\leftarrow e\in B_r^{\mathrm{str}}$ is evaluated by the engine. A
\emph{stochastic} binding
$b=f\leftarrow\texttt{@llm}(\pi_b,\,e_b)\ \texttt{@check}\ \chk_b\in B_r^{\mathrm{sto}}$
targets a primitive attribute $f$; $\pi_b$ is a prompt, $e_b$ an OCL expression
over the rule's source variables, and $\chk_b$ a validator. A \emph{hybrid
hand-off} is a transformation whose rules are hybrid rules.
\end{definition}

Two design choices follow from the aim of making failures diagnosable. First,
each LLM call sees a declared input, its \emph{footprint}, rather than the
whole conversation. What a value was produced from is then known exactly and
can be recorded and replayed. Second, a validator may read data that the LLM
does not see; for example, a test validator may compare a test against the
goal's examples even when the LLM writes the test from the docstring. A version
stamp, finally, records the inputs a value was produced and checked from, so
that the value can be recomputed when, and only when, these inputs change.

\begin{definition}[Footprint, validator reads, version stamp]\label{def:footprint}
Let $b$ be a stochastic binding of rule $r$ and $m$ a match of $r$. A
\emph{location} is a pair (object, feature) of a source model. The
\emph{footprint} $\fp_b(m)$ is the set of locations that the expression $e_b$
navigates for $m$; they are the only values the LLM may see. The
\emph{validator reads} $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$ are the locations that $\chk_b$
inspects besides the candidate value; $\chk_b$ is a predicate over the
candidate value and the values at $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$. The \emph{version stamp}
of an accepted value is the digest $\#(\fp_b(m)\cup\mathit{vr}_b(m))$ of the
values at all locations its production and its check read.
\end{definition}

A value written by an LLM must not decide which objects exist; otherwise the
structure of a hand-off would itself become random. We therefore require
\emph{stratification}: no guard and no structural binding may read a feature
that a stochastic binding writes. The condition is checked by \W{1}
(Section~\ref{sec:app-check}).

Our runtime departs from ATL in one respect. A reference binding whose expected
type lies in a \emph{source} view (for example \code{method <- m}, typed
\code{Goal.Method}) creates a cross-view link to the source object instead of
being resolved through the trace, because agents' views refer to the goal by
design. ATL would resolve \code{m} to the target that \code{m}'s own rule
created, which here has the wrong type; we apply that resolution only to
references whose type lies in the \emph{target} view. In addition, trace models
are persisted, as in MeTAGeM-Trace~\cite{vara2014metagem}.

We can now state what a hybrid hand-off means. Because its values are sampled,
a hand-off cannot denote a single target model. It denotes the set of targets
that its validators would accept.

\begin{definition}[Semantics of a hybrid hand-off]\label{def:hsem}
Let the \emph{structural part} $T^{\mathrm{str}}$ of a hybrid hand-off $T$ be
$T$ with every stochastic binding removed. Given source models $\vec M$, the
\emph{skeleton} $T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)$ is computed by ATL's matching and
initialisation, with the cross-view links above, and is deterministic. Like a
relation of QVT Relations (QVT-R)~\cite{omg2016qvt,stevens2010qvt}, and unlike
an ATL function, $T$ denotes the \emph{set} of admissible targets
\[\llbracket T\rrbracket(\vec M)=\{M_t\mid M_t\sqsupseteq T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)\ \wedge\ \forall(m,b):\ \chk_b(M_t[m,b],\mathit{vr}_b(m))\},\]
where $M_t\sqsupseteq T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)$ means that $M_t$ extends the
skeleton by exactly one value $M_t[m,b]$ for each match $m$ and stochastic
binding $b$. We require $\mathit{tgt}(T)\notin\mathit{src}(T)$. An execution
of $T$ samples one element of this set, or \emph{escalates} the bindings for
which no admissible value was found. An escalated execution lies outside
$\llbracket T\rrbracket$ and makes the run fail; an escalation is a recorded
fault, not a hand-over to a person.
\end{definition}

A team of hand-offs denotes the set of tuples of models, one per view, in which
every hand-off's target is admissible for its sources. On an acyclic hand-off
graph, such tuples are obtained hand-off by hand-off in topological order. The
footprint does not appear in $\llbracket T\rrbracket$: it constrains how a
value is produced, not which values are admissible, and the runtime enforces it
(Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}).

\begin{lstlisting}[float=tb, language=ATL, style=model, caption={The three hybrid hand-offs of the admitted typed \devteam{}. \code{@llm} marks a stochastic binding (second argument: footprint); \code{@check} its validator (Table~\ref{tab:validators}).}, label={lst:rules}]
rule Method2Design {                             -- Goal -> Design (owner: Architect)
  from m : Goal!Method
  to   d : Design!MethodDesign ( name <- m.name, method <- m,
         contract <- @llm('State a contract', Sequence{m.name, m.signature, m.docstring})
         @check nonempty(contract) ) }
rule Method2Test {                               -- Goal -> Test (owner: Tester)
  from m : Goal!Method
  to   t : Test!TestCase ( name <- m.name, method <- m,       -- structural; cross-view link
         code <- @llm('Write unit tests for this method',
                      Sequence{m.signature, m.docstring, m.examples.call, m.examples.expected})
         @check test_valid(code, m) ) }                        -- behaviour validator
rule Design2Impl {                               -- Design, Test -> Code (owner: Developer)
  from d : Design!MethodDesign, t : Test!TestCase (t.method = d.method)
  to   i : Code!MethodImpl ( name <- d.name, method <- d.method,
         body <- @llm('Implement the method',
                      Sequence{d.method.signature, d.contract, t.code})
         @check compiles(body) and passes_tests(body, t.code) ) }
\end{lstlisting}

In the running example, Listing~\ref{lst:rules} shows the three hand-offs of
the admitted \devteam{}. In \code{Method2Test}, the engine decides which test
cases exist and which method each one belongs to: one per goal method. The LLM
writes only the code, from the method's signature, docstring and examples. No
LLM can therefore forget to test \code{mark\_done}. The binding
\code{method <- m} is a cross-view link to the goal method, and the footprint
of \code{code} contains the four listed paths.

\subsection{What the builder proposes: typed teams}\label{sec:app-typed}

\tool{} executes a team but does not design one. In \auto{}, a builder LLM
writes the views, rules and validators, so they become an artefact that must be
checked. A free-form builder outputs role prompts
(Listing~\ref{lst:freeteam}); an \auto{} builder must output a \emph{typed
team}. Each component of a typed team makes explicit one aspect of the
specification that a composition defect concerns: the views and hand-offs fix
what is handed over (\D{2}), write rights fix who owns which artefact (\D{3}),
goal obligations fix what must be covered (\D{1}), the acceptance predicate
fixes how completion is decided (\D{4}), and the tool assignment fixes who can
do what (\D{5}).

\begin{definition}[Typed team]\label{def:typed}
A typed team is a tuple $\Team=(A,V,\mathbf{T},\omega,\kappa,G,\delta,\varphi)$
with the following components.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item $A$: a set of agents, each with a free-text role prompt.
\item $V=\{\MM_0,\dots,\MM_n\}$: a set of views with goal view $\MM_0$.
\item $\mathbf{T}$: a set of hybrid hand-offs, each with source views
$\mathit{src}(T)\subseteq V$ and one target view $\mathit{tgt}(T)\neq\MM_0$.
\item $\omega:A\to2^V$: the \emph{write rights} of each agent.
\item $\kappa:A\to2^{\mathit{Tools}}$: the tools of each agent.
\item $G$: a set of \emph{goal obligations} $(C,s,\mu,F_C)$. Each names a goal
class $C$, a \emph{scope} predicate $s$ that selects the objects of $C$ it
concerns, a set of \emph{anchor features} $F_C$ of $C$ (by default, all
attributes of $C$), and a mode $\mu$. Mode $\mathit{checked}$ requires that the
anchor features of every in-scope object reach a behavioural check; mode
$\mathit{delivered}$ requires a deliverable for every in-scope object.
\item $\delta=(D,g)$: the \emph{deliverable feature}, a target class $D$ and
its feature $g$ whose values form the deliverable.
\item $\varphi$: the proposed \emph{acceptance predicate}, a conjunction of
clauses that the engine evaluates at the end of a run
(Definition~\ref{def:accept}).
\end{itemize}
The goal view $\MM_0$ and the goal model $M_0$ are produced by the lifter; the
builder cannot change them.
\end{definition}

The \emph{owners} of a hand-off $T$ are the agents whose write rights include
its target view, $\mathit{owner}(T)=\{a\mid\mathit{tgt}(T)\in\omega(a)\}$; a
stochastic binding's owners are those of its hand-off. In an admitted team,
\W{2} makes the owner unique (Section~\ref{sec:app-check}).

\paragraph{Restricted expressions and the validator library} To keep every
admission condition decidable, a builder cannot write arbitrary OCL. Guards are
conjunctions of comparisons between navigation paths and literals; structural
bindings and footprints are navigation paths or literals; and validators are
chosen from a library rather than written. Each library entry declares a
\emph{strength}, either \emph{form} (the value parses, compiles or conforms) or
\emph{behaviour} (the value is executed against an oracle or a test); the tools
it needs; and which of its arguments it \emph{reads}. For a binding $b$, the
read arguments determine the validator reads $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$, and the
declared tools determine the tools $\tau(b)$ that the binding's owner needs;
$\tau(b)$ is derived, not proposed. Table~\ref{tab:validators} lists the
entries used in this paper.

\subsection{Running an admitted team}\label{sec:app-runtime}

Given an admitted typed team, the runtime executes its hand-offs, records what
every value was produced from, and decides at the end whether the run is done.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:handoff} executes one hand-off. Line~1 performs ATL's
matching and initialisation, made incremental~\cite{jouault2010incremental},
and creates the skeleton. Lines~2--6 sample values only for stochastic bindings
whose stamp is missing or \emph{stale}, that is, differs from the current
digest $\#(\fp_b(m)\cup\mathit{vr}_b(m))$. Each sample is checked by the
binding's validator; after $k$ rejections the binding escalates. The runtime
repeats Algorithm~\ref{alg:handoff} over all hand-offs in topological order
until no model changes.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\tool{}: executing one hybrid hand-off $T$ (runtime)}\label{alg:handoff}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require hand-off $T$; sources $\vec{M}$; target $M_t$; trace model $\TL_T$; samples per binding $k$
\State compute $T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)$ incrementally; update $M_t$ and $\TL_T$; $\mathit{Esc}\gets\emptyset$ \Comment{structure only}
\For{each stochastic binding $(t,b)$ of a match $m$ whose stamp is missing or stale}
  \For{$i=1$ \textbf{to} $k$}
    \State $v\gets\textsc{LLM}_{\mathit{owner}(T)}(\pi_b,\fp_b(m))$ \Comment{the prompt contains the footprint only}
    \If{$\chk_b(v,\mathit{vr}_b(m))$} accept $v$; update $\mathit{stamp}(t,b)$; \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{no value was accepted} $\mathit{Esc}\gets\mathit{Esc}\cup\{(t,b)\}$ \Comment{escalate} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $M_t,\TL_T,\mathit{Esc}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Agents at run time} An agent is a role prompt, a model and a set of
tools $\kappa(a)$. Agents do not converse. Each stochastic binding is one call
to its owner's LLM, whose prompt consists of the role prompt, $\pi_b$ and the
footprint; producing a value uses no tools. Tools are used only by validators,
which run with exactly the tools $\kappa(a)$ of the binding's owner. Agents
therefore see each other's work only through footprints, which is what makes a
recorded footprint sufficient for replay. Behaviour validators judge a value in
the context of the deliverable assembled from all values accepted so far, so
that a method is tested together with its siblings; Section~\ref{sec:impl}
describes how the deliverable is assembled.

\paragraph{Incremental re-execution} When the team or its inputs change, only
the affected values need to be produced again. Let a change $\Delta$ modify the
locations $\mathit{loc}(\Delta)$. A pair $(t,b)$ of a target object and a
stochastic binding becomes \emph{stale} if its footprint or validator reads
contain a location in $\mathit{loc}(\Delta)$, or a location written by a pair
that is already stale. The \emph{stale set} $\Stale(\Delta)$ is the least set of
pairs, over the trace models of all hand-offs, that is closed under these two
rules. All other values keep their stamps, so re-establishing the acceptance
predicate costs at most $k\cdot|\Stale(\Delta)|$ LLM calls. This is an
analytical property of the runtime; we use it for repair
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}) but do not evaluate arbitrary source changes in
this paper.

In the running example, if E2.2 is changed to expect a \code{KeyError}, the
stale set contains the test code of \code{mark\_done} and, one step later, its
implementation; all other values keep their stamps.

\paragraph{Deciding completion} Defect \D{4} arises when ``done'' is a claim.
In \auto{}, the engine instead decides completion from the trace. To state that
a goal object has been covered, we need a notion of what was derived from it,
including objects that were only read through a footprint. Run records provide
it.

\begin{definition}[Run record, descendant]\label{def:record}
At run time, every trace link $(m,t,r)$ carries, for each stochastic binding
$b$ of $r$, a \emph{run record} $(b,\fp_b(m),\mathit{vr}_b(m),\mathit{stamp},v)$
with the accepted value $v$ or an escalation mark. The link \emph{connects} to
each object in $t$ every object of $m$ and every object that owns a location in
a recorded footprint or validator read. An object $o'$ is a \emph{descendant}
of $o$ if a chain of connections leads from $o$ to $o'$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Engine clauses, acceptance]\label{def:accept}
The \emph{engine clauses} are evaluated on the final models and run records:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item $\mathit{cover}(G)$ holds if every goal object $o$ in the scope of an
obligation $(C,s,\mu,F_C)$ has a descendant that holds an accepted value of a
behaviour-validated binding (if $\mu=\mathit{checked}$) or of the deliverable
feature (if $\mu=\mathit{delivered}$);
\item $\mathit{valid}$ holds if every accepted value passes its validator on the
final models;
\item $\mathit{fresh}$ holds if every stamp equals the digest of the current
values; and
\item $\mathit{noEsc}$ holds if no binding is escalated.
\end{itemize}
The acceptance predicate of an admitted team consists of all four engine
clauses and, optionally, \emph{library clauses} that the engine evaluates on the
deliverable, such as running its public examples (\W{5}). A run is
\emph{done} iff $\varphi$ holds.
\end{definition}

The clause $\mathit{cover}(G)$ is deliberately one-sided: it asks only that a
checked value descends from the goal object. That both the producer and the
check of that value read the goal is ensured statically, by \W{4}.

In the running example, each \cls{Example} has as descendant the test case
whose footprint read it, although no rule matches \cls{Example}. Because no
agent output can end the run, a Tester whose test for E2.2 fails cannot cause
the team to be declared done.

\subsection{Admission: six well-formedness conditions}\label{sec:app-check}

The checker decides whether a typed team may run. It evaluates six conditions
on the typed team alone, without task data, LLM calls or execution.
\W{1}--\W{3} adapt to LLM-authored teams the checks that static analysers
perform on hand-written ATL transformations~\cite{cuadrado2017static};
\W{4}--\W{6} concern the team as a whole. Two graphs support them. The
\emph{hand-off graph} has an edge $\MM_i\to\MM_j$ for every hand-off with
$\MM_i\in\mathit{src}(T)$ and $\MM_j=\mathit{tgt}(T)$. The second graph tracks
data flow between features and is needed for coverage, as we explain first.

\paragraph{Why coverage needs feature-level data flow} The natural way to check
coverage is at the level of classes: require a path in the hand-off rules from
each goal class to some behaviour-checked class. Such a \emph{path-only} check
is not enough. A builder can create one test case per method, written from the
method's \emph{name} only. A path from \cls{Method} to a checked class then
exists, yet no example ever influences a check. Coverage must therefore be
checked at the level of features, and it must consider two roles that goal
data can play: input to the producer of a value, and input to that value's
check.

\begin{definition}[Feature-level data flow]\label{def:dataflow}
The data-flow graph $\mathcal{F}_\Team$ has a node $(C,f)$ for every class $C$
and feature $f$ of every view. A binding that writes feature $g$ of class $D$
contributes a \emph{production} edge $(C,f)\to_p(D,g)$ for every $(C,f)$ that
its expression or footprint reads, and a \emph{check} edge $(C,f)\to_c(D,g)$
for every $(C,f)$ that its validator reads. Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the set of
features written by stochastic bindings whose validator has behavioural
strength.
\end{definition}

A production edge transmits data: the producer sees it. A check edge transmits
nothing; it only gates whether the value is accepted. For a goal to be really
covered, the producer of a checked value must have seen the goal, \emph{and}
its check must test against the goal.

\begin{definition}[Anchoring]\label{def:anchor}
Write $\to_p^*$ for a path of zero or more production edges. A goal obligation
$(C,s,\mathit{checked},F_C)$ is \emph{anchored} at $(D,g)\in\mathcal{B}$ if,
for every anchor feature $f\in F_C$,
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{production side:} $(C,f)\to_p^*\,\to_p(D,g)$, and
\item \emph{check side:} $(C,f)\to_p^*(C',f')\to_c(D,g)$ for some node $(C',f')$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In the running example, the path \code{m.examples.call} in the footprint of
\code{Method2Test} reads both \code{Method.examples} and \code{Example.call}, so
it contributes production edges from both to \code{TestCase.code}; and
\code{test\_valid} reads \code{m.examples.*}, which contributes check edges.
Figure~\ref{fig:w4} shows the resulting graphs for the admitted team and for the
first proposal. The \emph{one-sided} variant that requires only the check side
(ii) is weaker: Section~\ref{sec:res-prev} shows that it admits teams whose
producers never see the goal.

\paragraph{The six conditions} Each condition below names the defect it
targets, states what it requires, and, where useful, notes its limits. \D{2}
is split between two conditions: \W{1} rules out its formal part (types and
paths) and \W{3} its content part (what a target must contain).

\paragraph{\W{1} Hand-offs are well typed and stratified (against the formal part of \D{2})}
Every rule reads only its source views and writes only features that its target
class declares; every guard, binding and footprint path type-checks; every
stochastic binding targets a primitive attribute and uses a library validator
with correctly typed arguments; and the team is stratified
(Section~\ref{sec:app-handoffs}).

\paragraph{\W{2} Every artefact has one writer (against \D{3})} The write
rights $\omega(a)$ are pairwise disjoint and together cover
$V\setminus\{\MM_0\}$, so $\mathit{owner}(T)$ is a single agent; and every
class is created by at most one rule, so no object has two producers. This is
stronger than ATL's requirement that each source tuple be matched by at most
one rule. The condition is conservative: rules with provably disjoint guards
could safely share a target class.

\paragraph{\W{3} Targets are complete (against the content part of \D{2})}
Every mandatory feature of a created class is bound exactly once. Every
reference binding whose type lies in the target view is \emph{resolvable}: some
rule creates an object of the expected class from the class of the source
object that the binding yields. Every reference binding whose type lies in a
source view yields objects of that type.

\paragraph{\W{4} Every goal is anchored (against \D{1})} \W{4} has four parts.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item Every obligation of mode \emph{checked} is anchored at some feature of
$\mathcal{B}$ (Definition~\ref{def:anchor}).
\item Every source-pattern class of a rule on an anchoring path is
\emph{total}: it is a goal class, or it is created by a rule whose guard is
implied by the obligation's scope. Totality ensures that the rule's other source
variables can be filled for every in-scope goal object.
\item Every obligation of mode \emph{delivered} reaches the deliverable feature
$\delta$ along production edges.
\item Every view is reachable from $\MM_0$ in the hand-off graph and reaches a
view that contains a feature of $\mathcal{B}$, so no agent is idle and no
agent's work goes unverified.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{\W{5} The engine decides completion (against \D{4})} $\varphi$
contains all four engine clauses of Definition~\ref{def:accept} and, apart from
them, only library clauses; every clause is evaluated by the engine, and no
clause may be an agent's claim. In addition, the hand-off graph is acyclic, so
that hand-offs can be executed in topological order
(Definition~\ref{def:hsem}).

\paragraph{\W{6} Work goes to agents that can do it (against \D{5})} For every
stochastic binding $b$, $\tau(b)\subseteq\kappa(a)$ for its owner $a$. Because
validators run with exactly the owner's tools, an agent without \code{exec}
cannot run the oracle that decides whether its value is accepted, just as a
Gaia role cannot act outside its permissions~\cite{wooldridge2000gaia}. When
\W{2} fails and a view has several writers, \W{6} is evaluated for each of
them.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Admit}: deciding whether a typed team $\Team$ may run}\label{alg:admit}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require typed team $\Team=(A,V,\mathbf{T},\omega,\kappa,G,\delta,\varphi)$; validator library
\State $\mathit{diag}\gets\textsc{TypeCheck}(\mathbf{T},V)\cup\textsc{Stratification}(\mathbf{T})$ \Comment{\W{1}}
\State $\mathit{diag}\gets\mathit{diag}\cup\textsc{Writers}(\omega,\mathbf{T})\cup\textsc{Completeness}(\mathbf{T})$ \Comment{\W{2}, \W{3}}
\State build $\mathcal{F}_\Team$ with production and check edges; $\mathcal{B}\gets$ behaviour-checked features
\For{each $(C,s,\mu,F_C)\in G$ that is not anchored with total source classes (or, if delivered, does not reach $\delta$)}
  \State $\mathit{diag}\gets\mathit{diag}\cup\{(\W{4},(C,s,\mu,F_C))\}$ \Comment{\W{4}(a)--(c)}
\EndFor
\State $\mathit{diag}\gets\mathit{diag}\cup\{(\W{4},V')\mid V'\text{ unreachable from }\MM_0\text{ or not reaching }\mathcal{B}\}$ \Comment{\W{4}(d)}
\State $\mathit{diag}\gets\mathit{diag}\cup\textsc{EngineClauses}(\varphi)\cup\textsc{Cycles}(\mathbf{T})$ \Comment{\W{5}}
\State $\mathit{diag}\gets\mathit{diag}\cup\{(\W{6},b)\mid\tau(b)\not\subseteq\kappa(a),\ a\text{ writes }\mathit{tgt}(b)\}$ \Comment{\W{6}; all writers if \W{2} fails}
\State \Return $\mathit{diag}$ \Comment{empty iff $\Team$ is admitted}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:admit} returns diagnostics that name the violated condition,
the offending element (a rule, a path, a write right or a clause) and a hint. Unlike
an LLM critic's comments, the diagnostics report \emph{every} violation of the
checked conditions, not only the first, and they point to a concrete element
rather than advising the builder to ``improve coordination''.

\paragraph{Properties} The checker runs on every candidate inside the builder's
loop (Section~\ref{sec:app-build}), so its cost matters.

\begin{proposition}[Cost]\label{prop:cost}
\W{1}--\W{6} are decidable on $\Team$ in time polynomial in $|\Team|$
(classes, features, rules, bindings and path segments).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
\W{1} navigates each path once; \W{2}, \W{3} and \W{6} compare finite sets.
\W{4}(a) and (c) are reachability problems over $\mathcal{F}_\Team$, whose size
is bounded by path segments times bindings, extended by one check edge;
implication between guards and scopes is tested on the restricted guard
language. \W{4}(d) and the cycle test of \W{5} are searches on the hand-off
graph.
\end{proof}

A second property explains why admission makes failures easier to locate. We
would like a failed $\mathit{cover}(G)$ clause never to stem from missing
structure, so that it always points at a value an LLM was asked to produce.
The property needs one more notion. A guard that equates two structural
references to the same goal object is a \emph{join}; in the running example,
\code{t.method = d.method} in \code{Design2Impl} is a join.

\begin{proposition}[Structural coverage]\label{prop:struct}
Let $\Team$ be admitted, and let every guard of the rules on the paths that
anchor an obligation $(C,s,\mathit{checked},F_C)$ either be implied by $s$ or
be a join. Then, for every object $o$ of class $C$ that satisfies $s$, the
skeletons $T^{\mathrm{str}}$ create a chain of descendants of $o$ that ends in
a target object $t$ with a behaviour-checked feature, whose footprint and validator
reads both contain data derived from $o$. Consequently, if
$\mathit{cover}(G)$ fails for $o$ at the end of a run, some stochastic binding
on that chain is escalated or invalid.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
By \W{1} (stratification), skeletons do not depend on LLM-written values, so
matches depend only on goal data and on objects created by earlier skeletons.
We proceed by induction along an anchoring path of
Definition~\ref{def:anchor}. \emph{Hypothesis:} after the skeletons of the
first $i$ rules on the path have run, the object that owns the $i$-th node
exists and is a descendant of $o$. \emph{Step:} the $(i{+}1)$-th edge stems
from a rule whose source pattern contains that object, or an object from which
the rule's footprint navigates to it. (1)~This source variable is filled by the
hypothesis. (2)~The other source variables are filled because their classes are
total (\W{4}(b)). (3)~The rule's guard holds, either because $s$ implies it or
because it is a join whose two sides were linked to the same goal object by
structural bindings. (4)~The rule therefore fires and, by \W{3}, creates its
target with all mandatory features. Hence every object on the chain exists, and
only stochastic values can be missing. By Algorithm~\ref{alg:handoff}, a
missing or rejected value is escalated, or reported by $\mathit{valid}$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:struct} turns locating the symptom of a coverage failure
into a lookup: such a failure is never a silent structural gap but points at a
named binding.

\paragraph{Limits} Whether a guard excludes in-scope objects is undecidable in
general. The checker accepts guards that are implied by the obligation's scope,
warns about all others, and $\mathit{cover}(G)$ enforces coverage at run time.
Coverage is also relative to what is declared: an obligation that the builder
does not declare is not checked (Section~\ref{sec:res-prev} shows the
consequence for deliverables). Admission does \emph{not} guarantee that the
views capture what the task needs, that validators are strong enough, or that
agents reason well.

\subsection{Building a team under the checker}\label{sec:app-build}

A builder that simply proposes a team and revises it as a whole tends to
repeat its own mistakes: in our pilots, a small builder often repeated an
earlier diagnostic in its next revision (Section~\ref{sec:res-prev} reports an
ablation).
\textsc{Build} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:build}) avoids this while keeping the
division of labour: the LLM proposes and revises, and only
Algorithm~\ref{alg:admit} admits.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Build}: proposing and revising a typed team under the checker (steps A--C)}\label{alg:build}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require task $x$; goal model $M_0$; budgets $k_{\mathit{prop}}$ (proposals), $k_{\mathit{rev}}$ (revisions per round), $k_{\mathit{adm}}$ (rounds)
\State $\mathcal{C}\gets\emptyset$
\For{$i=1$ \textbf{to} $k_{\mathit{prop}}$} \Comment{Step A, constrained to the typed-team format}
  \State $\Team_i\gets\textsc{Propose}(x,M_0)$;\ \ $\mathcal{C}\gets\mathcal{C}\cup\{(\Team_i,\textsc{Admit}(\Team_i))\}$ \Comment{Step B, Alg.~\ref{alg:admit}}
  \If{$\textsc{Admit}(\Team_i)=\emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $(\Team,\mathit{diag})\gets\arg\min_{(\Team',d)\in\mathcal{C}}|d|$ \Comment{the checker ranks the candidates}
\For{$r=1$ \textbf{to} $k_{\mathit{adm}}$} \Comment{Step C}
  \If{$\mathit{diag}=\emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State $\mathcal{P}\gets\textsc{Fragments}(\Team,\mathit{diag})$;\ \ $\Pi\gets\textsc{Paths}(\Team,\mathcal{P})$ \Comment{typed paths}
  \State $\mathcal{R}\gets\{\Team\lhd\textsc{RepairFragments}(\Team,\mathit{diag},\mathcal{P},\Pi)\mid 1..k_{\mathit{rev}}\}$ \Comment{$\lhd$: replace fragments only}
  \If{$\min_{\Team'\in\mathcal{R}}|\textsc{Admit}(\Team')|\ge|\mathit{diag}|$}
    \State $\mathcal{R}\gets\mathcal{R}\cup\{\textsc{Revise}(\Team,\mathit{diag})\}$ \Comment{fallback: whole-team revision}
  \EndIf
  \State $\Team'\gets\arg\min_{\Team''\in\mathcal{R}}|\textsc{Admit}(\Team'')|$
  \If{$|\textsc{Admit}(\Team')|\le|\mathit{diag}|$} $\Team\gets\Team'$;\ \ $\mathit{diag}\gets\textsc{Admit}(\Team')$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $(\Team,\mathit{diag})$ \Comment{$\mathit{diag}=\emptyset$ iff $\Team$ is admitted}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The builder relies on the typed-team format and on the checker in three ways.
First, every proposal and revision is constrained to the typed-team format, so
a candidate always has the \emph{shape} of a typed team; whether it is well
typed, complete and anchored remains the checker's decision. Second, because a
check is cheap (Section~\ref{sec:res-prev}), the builder samples up to
$k_{\mathit{prop}}$ proposals and lets the checker rank them by their number of
diagnostics. Third, the reviser does not regenerate the team. \textsc{Fragments}
maps each diagnostic to the parts of the team it concerns: a rule together with
the classes it creates and reads and the sources of its hand-off, a view, the
write rights $\omega$, the agents' tools $\kappa$, the obligations $G$ and the
deliverable $\delta$, or $\varphi$. For an unanchored goal, the fragments also
include the rules and views that should carry it. \textsc{Paths} lists, for
each diagnosed rule, short navigation paths that type-check from its source
variables. The reviser rewrites only these fragments, and the operator $\lhd$
puts them back into $\Team$ deterministically. A part that was not diagnosed is
therefore never regenerated, and a fix cannot be undone by rewriting the whole
team. A whole-team revision is tried only when no localised revision reduces
the number of violations.

\subsection{Attribution and checked repair}\label{sec:app-attr}

Transcript-based methods identify the responsible agent of a failure in about
half of the cases and the decisive step in about one in seven
(Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). \auto{} separates two questions. \emph{Where}
does a failure show? This is the \emph{symptom} binding, and the trace answers
it by lookup. \emph{Why} did it fail? This is the \emph{cause}, which may lie
in another binding, and a bounded replay answers it.

\paragraph{Locate} If $\varphi$ fails, the engine knows which clauses fail
and, through the trace models, where. A failed $\mathit{valid}$ clause or an
escalation names one pair $(t,b)$: a target object and a stochastic binding,
and with them its rule, hand-off and owner, and the stamped footprint its LLM
saw. A failed $\mathit{cover}(G)$ clause names, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}, a binding on the anchoring chain. Locating the
symptom is therefore a lookup, not an inference over a transcript. Attribution
needs the input of each step~\cite{chen2026traceelephant}; in \tool{}, that
input is a declared, bounded footprint.

\paragraph{Classify} The symptom binding is not always the cause. We
distinguish five fault classes, each with its remedy:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \textsc{Validator}: the check itself is unreliable. Remedy: replace the
validator (builder).
\item \textsc{Sampling}: the composition is adequate, but the owner's LLM was
unlucky or too weak. Remedy: retry, or use a stronger model.
\item \textsc{Footprint}$(j)$: the LLM did not see information it needed, an
instance of \D{2}. Remedy: widen the footprint by the paths, of at most $j$
references, that make replays pass.
\item \textsc{Upstream}$(b')$: an input of the binding, produced by another
stochastic binding $b'$, is wrong. Remedy: re-sample $b'$, after attributing
it in turn.
\item \textsc{Specification}: no value that the footprint supports satisfies
the prompt and the validator. Remedy: a builder delta.
\end{itemize}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} tests these classes in a fixed order. The validator's
stability is tested first, because it needs no LLM call;
\textsc{Specification} is the default when no other test succeeds.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Attribute}: classifying a failed stochastic binding $(t,b)$}\label{alg:attr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require failed value $v$ of $b$; recorded footprint $\phi$; replay cap $n_{\mathit{rep}}$; hop limit $h$
\If{$\chk_b(v,\mathit{vr}_b)$ is unstable over reruns} \Return \textsc{Validator} \EndIf
\If{\textsc{Replays}$(b,\phi)$ pass sometimes} \Return \textsc{Sampling} \EndIf
\For{$j=1$ \textbf{to} $h$}
  \If{\textsc{Replays}$(b,\phi\cup\textsc{Widen}_j(\phi))$ pass sometimes} \Return \textsc{Footprint}$(j)$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\For{each value $u\in\phi$ written upstream by a stochastic binding $b'$}
  \If{re-sampled $u$ makes \textsc{Replays}$(b,\phi)$ pass} \Return \textsc{Upstream}$(b')$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return \textsc{Specification} \Comment{no supported value satisfies $\chk_b$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The algorithm uses two operations. \textsc{Replays}$(b,\phi)$ re-samples $b$ on
footprint $\phi$ up to $n_{\mathit{rep}}$ times, one sample at a time, and
``passes sometimes'' if at least one sample passes $\chk_b$; under the
\emph{adaptive} replay budget it stops at the first pass.
\textsc{Widen}$_j(\phi)$ adds to $\phi$ every path from the rule's source
variables that follows at most $j$ references and ends in an attribute; a
footprint repair keeps only those added paths without which no replay passes.
The stability test of line~1 reruns the validator on the failed value, which
costs no LLM call. An \textsc{Upstream}$(b')$ result is followed by attributing
$b'$, with its accepted value treated as failed; in that recursive call, a
replay of $b'$ counts as passing only if the downstream binding $b$ then passes
its validator.
% TODO(author): confirm this pass criterion for the recursive call; the end-to-end example relies on it.
Section~\ref{sec:impl} gives the values of $n_{\mathit{rep}}$
and $h$ used in our evaluation.

\begin{remark}[Cost of attribution]\label{rem:attrcost}
For one binding $b$, Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} makes at most $n_{\mathit{rep}}$
replays for the sampling test, $h\cdot n_{\mathit{rep}}$ for the footprint
tests, and $|\mathit{up}(b)|$ re-samples plus $|\mathit{up}(b)|\cdot
n_{\mathit{rep}}$ replays for the upstream tests, where $\mathit{up}(b)$ is the
set of upstream stochastic values in its footprint: in total at most
$(h+1+|\mathit{up}(b)|)\,n_{\mathit{rep}}+|\mathit{up}(b)|$ LLM calls, plus the
replays that minimise a footprint repair. Recursion visits at most as many
bindings as the longest path in the hand-off graph.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Checked repair} A fault report becomes a team delta $\Delta$.
Sampling, footprint and upstream faults are repaired mechanically (retry, widen
the footprint, re-sample $b'$); validator and specification faults require a
delta proposed by the builder's LLM. $\Team\oplus\Delta$ denotes the typed team
with $\Delta$'s edits applied. The checker evaluates it with
Algorithm~\ref{alg:admit}, and \textsc{Apply} installs it in the running team
only if it is admitted. As with consistency-preserving
operators~\cite{horcas2023consistency,alomar2021preserving}, deltas differ in
what they preserve, and \textsc{Apply} uses one of three modes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{in place}, for a delta that changes only rules, prompts, footprints
or validators: the affected values lose their stamps and become stale, and all
other accepted values are kept;
\item \emph{by extension}, for a delta that only adds views, agents or
hand-offs: the compiler generates the new rule modules, so that new agents
receive work for everything already built;
\item \emph{rebuild}, for any other delta, as a free-form builder must always do.
\end{itemize}
Changes flow only from the team model to the runtime, never back: the runtime
is regenerated from the team model.

\paragraph{The running example, end to end} We follow \code{TaskBoard} from
the first proposal to an accepted deliverable.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Admission.} The first proposal (Listing~\ref{lst:proposal}) is
rejected with five diagnostics (Listing~\ref{lst:diag}). The reviser rewrites
only the named fragments: the hand-offs and views for \W{1} and \W{4}
(including the rules that should carry the unanchored goal), the write rights
for \W{2}, $\varphi$ for \W{5}, and the agents' tools for \W{6}. Its revision
replaces \code{Impl2Run} by \code{Method2Test} (Listing~\ref{lst:rules}), gives
the Tester \code{exec}, removes the Developer's write right on Test, adds
\code{m.docstring} to the footprint of \code{Method2Design}, replaces
\code{d.returnType} by \code{d.method.signature}, lets \code{Design2Impl} read
the Test view (joined on the method) with the tests in its footprint and a
\code{passes\_tests} validator, and drops the Tester's claim from $\varphi$.
The revised team is admitted.
\item \emph{Run.} The implementation of \code{mark\_done} escalates after
three samples fail \code{passes\_tests}.
\item \emph{Locate.} Lookup names the pair
$(\mathit{impl}_{\code{mark\_done}},\code{body})$, owned by the Developer.
\item \emph{Classify the implementation.} Replays on the same footprint fail,
and widening the footprint does not help. Re-sampling the upstream test code,
however, makes the replays pass. The reason is that the Tester's first test
called \code{mark\_done(1)} on a fresh board, without the \code{add\_task}
that E2.1 relies on, so every correct implementation failed it.
\code{test\_valid} had accepted that test because it runs, fails on a stub and
asserts every example. The fault is \textsc{Upstream}, in
$(\mathit{test}_{\code{mark\_done}},\code{code})$, owned by the Tester.
\item \emph{Classify the test.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} recurses on the test.
No replay of the test on its own footprint adds the missing setup. Widening the
footprint by two references adds \code{m.task.examples.*}, which contains E1.1,
and the replays then pass: a \textsc{Footprint}$(2)$ fault of
\code{Method2Test}.
\item \emph{Repair.} The mechanical delta widens that footprint, is admitted,
and is applied in place. Only the test and the implementation of
\code{mark\_done} become stale and are re-sampled, after which $\varphi$ holds.
\end{enumerate}
A stronger \code{test\_valid} that also checked the order of examples would
have rejected the first test.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}\label{sec:app-scope}

\auto{} checks that a team fits \emph{together}, not that its views capture the
task or that its validators are strong. Hand-off graphs are acyclic, LLMs fill
in attributes but do not create structure, and teams whose value lies in
open-ended dialogue do not decompose naturally into views and rules.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

We organise related work into seven lines. For each, we state what it
contributes and where \auto{} differs; Table~\ref{tab:rw} summarises the
comparison on the properties that matter for composition.

\subsection{Hand-designed LLM multi-agent teams for software engineering}\label{sec:rw-teams}

Role-based teams emulate human development processes. MetaGPT gives roles
structured output documents~\cite{hong2024metagpt}; ChatDev chains role pairs
through development phases~\cite{qian2024chatdev}; Self-Collaboration and
MapCoder split analysis, coding, testing and
debugging~\cite{dong2024selfcollab,islam2024mapcoder}; and AutoGen and
AgentVerse let developers wire arbitrary
topologies~\cite{wu2024autogen,chen2024agentverse}. Two surveys list
coordination and verification among the open
problems~\cite{he2025lma,liu2024agents4se}. These teams have a human architect.
Beyond the document types of MetaGPT, nothing checks before execution that
every requirement reaches a test, that each artefact has one writer, or that
the system rather than an agent decides completion. \auto{} provides these
properties for teams that no human reviews.

\subsection{Automatic team builders and learned orchestration}\label{sec:rw-builders}

\emph{Per-task builders} design a team for each task. CaptainAgent~\cite{song2024captain},
AutoAgents~\cite{chen2024autoagents} and MAS-Zero~\cite{ke2025maszero} rely on
reflector, observer or meta-feedback LLMs; EvoAgent evolves
agents~\cite{yuan2025evoagent}; DyLAN selects them~\cite{liu2024dylan}; and
MetaAgent represents the team as a finite state machine that makes control
explicit~\cite{zhang2025metaagent}. \emph{Offline builders} search for agentic
workflows as code or graphs: ADAS~\cite{hu2025adas}, AFlow~\cite{zhang2025aflow},
GPTSwarm~\cite{zhuge2024gptswarm}, MaAS~\cite{zhang2025maas} and the GNN-based
G-Designer~\cite{zhang2025gdesigner}. \emph{Learned orchestrators} are trained
with reinforcement learning to pick the next agent
(Puppeteer~\cite{dang2025puppeteer}), to write natural-language workflows
(Conductor~\cite{nielsen2026conductor}) or to emit whole teams
(MAS-Orchestra~\cite{ke2026masorchestra}); other work evolves agent graphs or
refines teams from textual feedback~\cite{yang2025agentnet,ma2026ann,he2026tpgo}.

All of these approaches optimise \emph{which} team to build against an
end-to-end score or another LLM's judgement. Code-based workflows are parsed
and executed, and MetaAgent checks its state machine for reachability. None,
however, states as a checkable interface what is handed over between each pair
of agents, and none checks data-flow coverage or the ownership of hand-offs
before the team runs. \auto{} is complementary: it proposes no new search
strategy but a target representation that any builder can emit, and a check
that a builder can run inside its loop in milliseconds, with diagnostics that
name a rule, path or write right.

\subsection{Failure analysis and attribution for multi-agent systems}\label{sec:rw-failures}

MAST derives 14 failure modes from more than 1{,}600 annotated
traces~\cite{cemri2025mast}, and AgentAsk locates errors on hand-off
edges~\cite{lin2026agentask}. Further studies analyse why agent frameworks
fail, how team structure must fit the task, and why communication does not
imply
coordination~\cite{lu2025exploring,kim2025scaling,pappu2026experts,zhang2026silobench,zhu2025multiagentbench,lamalfa2025miss}.
Who\&When and
TraceElephant attribute failures to agents and steps from
transcripts~\cite{zhang2025whowhen,chen2026traceelephant}. These works
characterise failures after the fact and from prose. \auto{} prevents a class
of them before execution and, when a typed team fails, locates the symptom by
lookup and classifies it by bounded replay. RQ1 builds on their taxonomies and
data, and RQ4 uses their attribution methods as baselines.

\subsection{Structured communication and typed LLM programming}\label{sec:rw-structured}

Agent communication languages gave messages explicit performatives and shared
ontologies long before LLMs~\cite{finin1994kqml}. For LLM agents, MCP and A2A
standardise the transport between agents and tools~\cite{mcp2024,a2a2025};
Agora lets agents negotiate protocol documents~\cite{marro2024agora}; and
PatchBoard grounds collaboration in schema-validated patches to a shared
state~\cite{zhang2026patchboard}. Constrained decoding guarantees that outputs
conform to a grammar or JSON schema~\cite{willard2023outlines}. Practitioner
frameworks type hand-offs too: LangGraph declares a typed state schema for a
graph of agents~\cite{langgraph2025}, CrewAI validates a task's output against
a Pydantic model~\cite{crewai2025}, and AgentSpec enforces user-written rules on
agent actions at run time~\cite{wang2026agentspec}.

These approaches make an individual \emph{message} or action well formed,
reliably transported or permitted. They do not check properties of the
\emph{assembled team}: that every obligation reaches a check that reads it,
that every artefact has one writer, that completion is decided by the system,
or that work goes to an agent with the needed tools. A single shared schema
also removes heterogeneity by decree, whereas \auto{} keeps one view per agent,
sized to its job, and records correspondences in trace models. We use a
PatchBoard-style shared schema as a baseline (Section~\ref{sec:design}).

Closest to the \tool{} runtime are LLM programming systems that interleave
deterministic control with generated parts: LMQL constrains and scripts
generation inside a query language~\cite{beurerkellner2023lmql}, SGLang
executes structured LM programs efficiently~\cite{zheng2024sglang}, and DSPy
composes LM modules whose inputs and outputs are typed by
signatures~\cite{khattab2024dspy}. These systems control \emph{how} one program
calls an LLM. \tool{} instead fixes \emph{which} values exist by
model-transformation rules. It gives each value a declared footprint, accepts
it only behind a validator, stamps it for incremental re-sampling, and lets the
engine decide completion; the checker and attribution rely on exactly these
properties.

\subsection{Static analysis, verification and testing of model transformations}\label{sec:rw-mt}

MDE has a mature body of work on checking individual transformations. Static
analysers for ATL detect typing errors, unresolved bindings, rule conflicts and
missing mandatory features~\cite{cuadrado2017static}; invariant-based and
model-finding approaches verify declarative transformations against
contracts~\cite{cabot2010invariants,rahim2015survey}; mutation analysis
assesses transformation test suites~\cite{mottu2006mutation}; and a survey
covers testing and debugging~\cite{troya2022survey}. Fault localisation ranks
suspicious rules statically or from test
spectra~\cite{burgueno2015static,troya2018sbfl}, and anATLyzer proposes
speculatively checked quick fixes~\cite{cuadrado2018quickfix}. Transformation
intents make the properties a transformation must satisfy
explicit~\cite{lucio2016intents}. \W{1}--\W{3} are deliberately close to
established static checks~\cite{cuadrado2017static}. Our diagnostics play the
role of anATLyzer's quick-fix hints, but the fixes are proposed by an LLM and
re-checked; and unlike spectrum-based localisation~\cite{troya2018sbfl}, which
ranks rules by test outcomes, attribution in \auto{} replays one binding on its
recorded footprint. We adopt mutation analysis~\cite{mottu2006mutation} to
evaluate the checker itself.

Closer to the \emph{team} level, transformation chains have been typed and
checked for the compatibility of their connected
metamodels~\cite{vanhooff2007uniti}, and chains between incompatible metamodels
have been assembled automatically~\cite{basciani2014chaining}; footprinting
estimates which parts of a model an operation reads~\cite{jeanneret2011footprints}.
This work assumes that a person writes the transformations and that their
execution is deterministic. Our footprints are \emph{declared} rather than
estimated. Chain typing asks whether a chain's metamodels are compatible;
anchored coverage (\W{4}) asks a finer question, namely whether specific
\emph{goal features} flow into both the production and the check of a value.
This question matters most when values are produced by a stochastic author and
accepted by validators, because then a value can pass its check without its
producer ever having seen the goal. Engine-decided completion (\W{5}) and
capability fit (\W{6}) concern the team rather than any single transformation.

\subsection{Large language models in model-driven engineering}\label{sec:rw-llm-mde}

LLMs have been evaluated for domain modelling~\cite{chen2023domainmodeling},
model completion~\cite{chaaben2023fewshot} and UML modelling
tasks~\cite{camara2023chatgptuml}, with useful but imperfect accuracy; an
evaluation of LLM-based domain-modelling assistance reaches a similar
conclusion~\cite{benchaaben2026utility}. Prompting itself can be model
driven~\cite{clariso2023mdpe}, LLMs have completed software model evolution
from edit histories~\cite{tinnes2025evolution}, and mapping studies chart LLM
use across MDE activities and identify LLM-written metamodels and
transformations as an open setting~\cite{dirocco2025llmmde,zhang2027llmmde}.
Recent studies ask LLMs to write transformations in model transformation
languages and report that syntactic and semantic errors remain
frequent~\cite{jiang2026llm4mtls,kazai2025mtllm}. This line evaluates
\emph{individual} LLM-generated models or transformations. \auto{} accepts that
a proposal will be imperfect and checks how the generated views and
transformations fit together; RQ2 measures how often an LLM reaches an
admissible team. Where an LLM performs a model transformation, it replaces the
transformation as a whole~\cite{kazai2025mtllm}; we are not aware of MDE work
in which rules delegate individual bindings to an LLM behind validators, as
\tool{} does.

\subsection{Software architecture and agent-oriented software engineering}\label{sec:rw-arch}

Architectural mismatch, design by contract and specified connectors make the
assumptions between components explicit and
checkable~\cite{garlan1995mismatch,garlan2009mismatch,meyer1992dbc,allen1997connection},
and Gaia gave every agent role responsibilities, permissions and
protocols~\cite{wooldridge2000gaia}. \auto{} transfers these remedies to
systems that an LLM composes without review: write rights play the role of
Gaia's permissions, hand-offs that of connectors, and $\varphi$ that of a
system-level postcondition.

\subsection{Research questions}\label{sec:rqs}

\auto{} changes how a team is built and run: the builder must write a typed
team, a checker decides whether the team may run, and a trace records how every
value was produced. Such machinery is not free. It costs tokens and time, and a
builder that cannot write an admissible team produces no team at all. The
evaluation therefore asks what an engineer \emph{gets} for this price. We
evaluate the two benefits that the design promises, and the mechanism that
delivers both. For each, we report the price next to the benefit.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.1em, style=nextline, itemsep=3pt]
\item[\RQ{1} (Reliability): When an \auto{} team reports that it is done, can
we believe it, and when it fails, does it say so?]
Every condition in our study has its own way of ending a run: an agent stops,
a team sends \code{TERMINATE}, or the engine finds the goal formula $\varphi$
true (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}, last column). A developer who uses a team
without re-checking every output relies on this signal. A failure that the
team reports as a success is the costly case, because nobody looks at it. We
call it a \emph{silent failure}. RQ1 compares, over all 44{,}352 runs, how often
each condition fails silently, how often a failed run is flagged as unfinished,
and how much a ``done'' raises the chance that the result is correct.
\emph{Price measured:} task success, how often a run reports ``done'' at all,
and tokens.

\item[\RQ{2} (Traceability): When an \auto{} team fails, can the failure be
traced to the responsible part of the team, explained and repaired there?]
In a free-form team, a failure leaves a chat transcript, and finding the
responsible agent means reading it. In \auto{}, every value has a trace link to
the rule, the agent and the inputs that produced it
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}). RQ2 injects known faults into running teams and
asks whether trace-based attribution finds the responsible binding and names
the kind of fault more accurately than four transcript-based methods from the
literature. It also asks whether a checked repair of the diagnosed part works
as well as rebuilding the whole team.
\emph{Price measured:} LLM calls and time spent on attribution.

\item[\RQ{3} (Prevention): Does the admission check catch composition defects
before the team runs, and what does it cost?]
Both benefits rest on the checker: RQ1's reliable ``done'' requires that the
goal is covered by executed checks (\W{4}, \W{5}), and RQ2's lookup requires
that the trace is well formed. RQ3 tests the checker on 151{,}516 mutants of
builder-generated teams, compares it with LLM critics on independently seeded
defects, and measures its run time.
\emph{Price measured:} sessions in which the builder never produces an
admissible team, and milliseconds of checking.
\end{description}

\noindent Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} then collects the price that matters most
to practitioners, task success, and reports a pre-registered experiment that
tested whether a larger budget closes the success gap. It does not.

\paragraph{What we do not claim} An earlier version of this study also asked
how often composition defects are the \emph{decisive} cause of a failure and
whether \auto{} reduces such failures. Answering this requires labelling the
cause of each failure. Our two LLM coders disagreed on the defect class in
65.6\% of 5{,}580 sampled failures (Cohen's $\kappa=0.16$), and we did not have
the resources for human coding at this scale. We therefore make no claim about
the causes of failures, and we drop the per-defect shares.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

This section describes how the concepts of Section~\ref{sec:approach} are
realised in the implementation that we evaluate.

\paragraph{Representation} The compiler and runtime are written in Python. Models are JSON object graphs, validated
against a JSON Schema that is generated from each view. Compiled rule modules
are data that the engine interprets. Guards, bindings and footprints use an
OCL fragment of navigation paths, literals, comparisons and conjunction.

\paragraph{Builder} A typed team is exchanged as JSON. The JSON Schema of this
format plays the role of the typed-team metamodel, and every proposal and
revision is decoded under it (steps A and C). The builder's prompt library
contains this schema, the validator library, three worked examples (Section~\ref{sec:design})
and, for revisions, the navigation paths produced by \textsc{Paths}, which
follow at most two references. The anchor features of an obligation default to
all attributes of its goal class.

\paragraph{Runtime} The prompt of a stochastic binding consists of the owner's
role prompt, $\pi_b$, and the footprint serialised as a list of
\emph{object.feature = value} lines. Validators run in a sandbox that grants
exactly the owner's tools $\kappa(a)$. The deliverable $\delta=(D,g)$ names a
target class and feature, here \code{MethodImpl.body}; the runtime assembles
the deliverable by inserting every value of $g$ into the class outline given by
the goal model $M_0$ (for function-level tasks, into the function signature).
Behaviour validators run on this assembled artefact. Methods without an
accepted value yet are replaced by stubs, and examples run in docstring order
on one object, as doctest does, so that E2.1 can rely on the task that E1.1
added.

\paragraph{Attribution} We use a replay cap of $n_{\mathit{rep}}=3$ with the
adaptive budget (stop at the first passing replay), a hop limit of $h=2$, and
three reruns of the validator for the stability test.


\subsection{Experimental design}\label{sec:design}

We follow guidelines for experiments in software
engineering~\cite{wohlin2012experimentation} and for empirical studies involving
LLMs~\cite{wagner2025guidelines,sallou2024breaking}: local open-weight models,
fixed versions and decoding parameters, several seeds, all prompts and logs
released, and cost reported alongside quality.

\paragraph{Subjects} \emph{ClassEval}~\cite{du2024classeval} contains 100
manually crafted Python class-level tasks with 412 methods and, on average, 33.1
hidden tests per class; a task succeeds only if \emph{all} hidden tests of the
class pass. Class-level tasks are where a team can plausibly help, because
methods interact and must be designed, implemented and tested consistently.
\emph{HumanEval+}~\cite{liu2023evalplus} contains the 164 HumanEval
problems~\cite{chen2021codex} with tests extended by EvalPlus. It represents
small, self-contained tasks for which a team should add little. Teams see only
the prompt, signatures, docstrings and public examples; hidden tests are used
only for scoring.

\paragraph{Models} We use seven instruction-tuned models from six families, all
run locally through Ollama with 4-bit weights (tags in the package):
Qwen2.5-Coder 7B and 32B~\cite{hui2024qwen25coder}, the agentic
Devstral 24B~\cite{rastogi2025devstral}, the general-purpose
Gemma~3 27B~\cite{gemmateam2025gemma3}, Granite-Code
20B~\cite{mishra2024granitecode}, Code Llama 13B~\cite{roziere2023codellama} and
the completion-oriented StarCoder2 15B~\cite{lozhkov2024starcoder2}. We chose
them to vary the ability to emit a long, schema-conforming specification. We had
planned to use DeepSeek-Coder-V2 16B~\cite{deepseek2024coderv2}; under Ollama
its generation stalled (requests timed out with a single output token), so we
replaced it with Gemma~3 for this technical reason, not because of its results.

same model.

\paragraph{Conditions} Table~\ref{tab:conditions} defines eight conditions.
Single is the reference that every team must beat. Single-Gate is
compute-matched to \auto{}: it samples until \auto{}'s median token budget is
spent and accepts a candidate only when the public examples pass. It therefore
also executes checks before declaring success, which lets us ask whether
\auto{}'s reliability comes from executing checks alone. Free, Critic and Schema
are re-implementations in the style of published multi-agent systems with the
same model, tools and examples, because those systems target other benchmarks
and proprietary models. The two ablations isolate parts of \auto{}: Typed-NC
keeps the typed \emph{format} but removes the \emph{check}, and Typed-Ref
replaces the \emph{builder} by a hand-written team.

\paragraph{Procedure} Every condition runs on every task of both benchmarks
with every model and three seeds: $8\times7\times264\times3=44{,}352$ runs, on
NVIDIA A100 GPUs of a national research cluster (Ollama version, model digests
and job scripts are in the package). Temperatures are 0.2 for agents and
bindings and 0.6 for the builder; outputs are capped at 4{,}096 tokens; the
runtime draws up to $k=3$ samples per binding; and the builder samples
$k_{\mathit{prop}}=3$ proposals and $k_{\mathit{rev}}=2$ revisions per round.
Builders receive three structurally different worked examples, with 2, 3 and 4
agents and none taken from either benchmark. In a pilot, one example's analyst
asked for ``edge cases and error conditions'', and the builders' teams copied
this into tests that demand exceptions the task never specifies. We reworded
that example, in both its typed and its prose rendering, to ask only for
documented behaviour, before the main runs.

\paragraph{Version of \auto{} and a post-hoc revision} All results in
Sections~\ref{sec:res-rel}--\ref{sec:res-prev} use the version of \auto{} that
was frozen before the main runs. After seeing them, we revised \auto{} to raise
its success (three changes, described in Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}). Because
the revision was designed with knowledge of the results, we evaluate it only on
tasks that were not used to design it (80\% of each benchmark, selected with a
fixed seed before the revision), in a pre-registered experiment, and we report
it separately. The baselines were not changed.

\paragraph{Metrics} \emph{Success}: all hidden tests pass. For RQ1 we compare
each condition's own completion signal (``says done'') with success:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Silent-failure rate}: the share of \emph{all} runs that say done but
fail. This is the rate at which a user who trusts the signal accepts a wrong
result.
\item \emph{Flagged failures}: the share of \emph{failed} runs that do not say
done, that is, failures the condition admits.
\item \emph{Precision of ``done''}: the share of runs that say done that pass.
\item \emph{Lift}: precision divided by the condition's success rate. A lift of
1 means that ``done'' carries no information; a lift of 4 means that a run that
says done is four times as likely to pass as a run of that condition chosen at
random.
\end{itemize}
The first two measures can be improved by a condition that rarely says done;
precision and lift cannot. We therefore read them together, and report how
often each condition says done. \emph{Cost}: input and output tokens and
wall-clock time per run. Attribution and detection measures are defined in
their sections.

\paragraph{Statistical analysis} Runs are clustered by task, because the same
task appears in every condition, model and seed. All intervals on proportions
are 95\% percentile intervals from a task-clustered bootstrap with 1{,}000
resamples~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}, unless stated otherwise; Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927} are used for small samples. Per model and
benchmark, success is compared by exact McNemar
tests~\cite{mcnemar1947} on task-level majority outcomes over the three seeds,
Holm-corrected within each family of seven comparisons~\cite{holm1979}. Paired
comparisons of attribution and repair use exact McNemar tests. The
pre-registered experiment of Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} uses logistic models
fitted by generalised estimating equations (GEE), clustered by
task~\cite{liang1986gee}. We use $\alpha=0.05$.

Table~\ref{tab:reliability} and Figure~\ref{fig:silent} summarise the results.
We report four findings.

\paragraph{(1) \auto{} has the fewest silent failures of all conditions} When
an \auto{} run says done, it is rarely wrong: 5.9\% of ClassEval runs and 5.6\%
of HumanEval+ runs fail silently. A single agent fails silently three to four
times as often (17.6\% and 20.4\%; risk ratios 0.34 and 0.27), and free-form,
critic and shared-schema teams five to ten times as often (27--56\%). The
intervals of \auto{} do not overlap with those of any baseline. The effect is
not an artefact of pooling. \auto{} has fewer silent failures than Single for six
of the seven models on both benchmarks; the seventh, StarCoder2, ties at zero
because its single agent never succeeds or says done. It has fewer silent
failures than all three multi-agent baselines in 12 of the 14 model--benchmark
pairs. The two exceptions are Devstral's Free and Critic teams, which rarely send
\code{TERMINATE} at all (per-model values are in the package).

\paragraph{(2) When \auto{} fails, it says so} Of the failed \auto{} runs,
93.4\% (ClassEval) and 90.9\% (HumanEval+) end without ``done'': the engine
reports an unmet obligation, an open escalation or a refusal instead of a
result. A failed single-agent run is flagged in 77\% and 48\% of cases, and a
failed multi-agent run in 32--48\%. For a developer this is the practical
difference: with \auto{}, a failure that needs attention is almost always
visible as one.

\paragraph{(3) A ``done'' from \auto{} carries the most information} A
condition could reach findings (1) and (2) by never saying done. \auto{} does
not do this: its ``done'' raises the chance of a correct result more than any
other condition's. On HumanEval+, 85.5\% of \auto{}'s ``done'' runs pass, the
highest precision of all conditions (single agent 74.5\%, multi-agent teams
56--63\%). On ClassEval, its precision (43.9\%) is as high as a single agent's
(47.7\%; the intervals overlap widely) and twice that of multi-agent teams
(19--22\%). Its lift is the highest on both benchmarks: a ClassEval run that
says done is 4.2 times as likely to pass as an average \auto{} run, against 2.0
for a single agent and 1.0--1.2 for multi-agent teams, whose ``done'' carries
almost no information.

\paragraph{(4) The reliability comes from the check, not from executing tests
alone} Two comparisons locate the source. First, \emph{Typed-NC} runs the same
typed format and engine without the checker. Removing the check increases
silent failures from 5.9\% to 10.1\% (ClassEval) and from 5.6\% to 12.7\%
(HumanEval+), lowers flagged failures from 93.4\% to 88.8\% and from 90.9\% to
80.2\%, and lowers precision from 43.9\% to 25.0\% and from 85.5\% to 68.7\%.
This suggests that teams which run unchecked declare completion on goals that
no check covers. Second, \emph{Single-Gate} executes the public examples
before it declares success and spends as many tokens as \auto{}, yet it fails
silently at least as often as Single (21.5\% on both benchmarks). Passing the public
examples is a weaker signal than $\varphi$, which requires that every declared
obligation has been checked (\W{4}, \W{5}). Executing \emph{some} checks is not
enough; the checker ensures that the checks cover the goal.

\paragraph{The price} The reliability of \auto{} is bought with lower success
and a more conservative ``done''. \auto{} says done in only 10.5\% (ClassEval)
and 38.4\% (HumanEval+) of runs, against 33.6\% and 80.1\% for a single agent.
Part of this is correct caution: a run that does not say done passes in only
6.7\% (ClassEval) and 9.9\% (HumanEval+) of cases. Part of it is lost work:
\auto{} succeeds in 10.6\% and 38.9\% of runs, against 24.5\% and 61.0\% for a
single agent (Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} explains why). \auto{} also spends 7
times (ClassEval) and 16 times (HumanEval+) the output tokens of a single agent,
and 14 times its input tokens on ClassEval (65.8k against 4.6k per run), because
every binding is prompted separately with its footprint.

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, colback=modelbg, colframe=modelbar, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=1pt, left=6pt, right=6pt, top=3pt, bottom=3pt]
\small\textbf{Answer to RQ1.} Yes. Of the eight conditions, \auto{} has the
fewest silent failures on both benchmarks (5.9\% and 5.6\% of runs, a third to a
quarter of a single agent's rate and a fifth or less of multi-agent teams'),
flags more than 90\% of its failures, and gives the most informative ``done''
(lift 4.2 and 2.2). The effect holds for six of seven models and disappears
when the checker is removed. \emph{Price:} \auto{} says done less often,
succeeds less often, and uses 7--16 times the output tokens of a single agent.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{RQ2: Can failures be traced, explained and repaired?}\label{sec:res-trace}

\paragraph{Setup} To know the true cause of a failure, we injected it. We took
runs that had succeeded and injected one fault from one of five classes:
\textsc{Upstream}, a wrong upstream value accepted by a weak validator;
\textsc{Footprint}, a footprint made too narrow;
\textsc{Specification}, an unsatisfiable validator;
\textsc{Sampling}, a hotter and smaller model on one agent, giving pass rates of
0.3--0.6; and
\textsc{Validator}, a flaky validator.
Each fault is injected into a snapshot of a clean run, re-sampling only what
the injection makes stale, so the faulted run differs from the clean run only by
the injection. A fault counts if $\varphi$ then fails at a value that the clean
run had accepted (for \textsc{Upstream}, at the value downstream of the
corrupted one). We injected faults into ClassEval runs of the hand-written
Typed-Ref team with Qwen2.5-Coder 7B and 32B and Devstral, and into runs of
builder-generated \auto{} teams with Qwen2.5-Coder 32B. Of 1{,}108 injections,
993 produced a failure (752 in reference teams, 241 in builder teams; 47--153
per class). Four transcript-based methods from Who\&When and
TraceElephant~\cite{zhang2025whowhen,chen2026traceelephant} (all at once, step
by step, binary search and agentic replay) received the same 752 faulted
reference-team runs as transcripts with full inputs, Qwen2.5-Coder 32B as
judge, and the five classes as a closed label set. For repair, we took 562
failed \auto{} runs and compared one checked repair of the diagnosed part
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}) with rebuilding the whole team from scratch with
the same fault report.

\paragraph{(1) The trace locates the failure and the responsible part}
Table~\ref{tab:attribution} reports the results. Trace lookup finds the
binding where a failure shows in every case, as
Proposition~\ref{prop:struct} guarantees. More importantly, it finds the binding
where the fault was \emph{injected} in 98.3\% of reference-team failures and
96.3\% of builder-team failures, including 92.5\% and 83.3\% of upstream faults,
whose symptom appears one hand-off later. The best transcript method locates the
responsible step in 61.8\% of cases. The responsible agent is found in 78\% of
cases by \auto{}, against at most 55\% by transcript methods. Results are
stable across the three models of the reference team (agent 76--79\%,
responsible binding 96--100\%).

\paragraph{(2) The trace explains what kind of fault occurred} With adaptive
replay, \auto{} names the fault class correctly in 75.3\% of reference-team
failures and 73.7\% of builder-team failures, more than twice the best
transcript method (32.8\%). Specification, upstream and footprint faults are
classified correctly in 83--100\% of cases. The hard case is telling
\textsc{Sampling} faults (an agent that fails by chance) apart from
\textsc{Validator} faults (a check that fails by chance): both make a replay
pass sometimes, so in reference teams 43\% of sampling faults are taken for
validator faults and 57\% of validator faults for sampling faults.
The replay budget matters for exactly this case. With a single replay,
sampling faults are recognised in 7\% of cases, because the injected agents fail
a single replay 40--70\% of the time; with up to three replays, in 57\%. Macro
accuracy rises from 65.7\% to 75.3\% (77 against 4 discordant failures, exact
McNemar $p<.001$).

\paragraph{(3) Repairing the diagnosed part works at least as well as
rebuilding, with less work} One checked repair made the task pass its hidden
tests in 21.5\% [18.3, 25.1] of the 562 failed runs, against 18.9\% [15.8, 22.3]
for rebuilding the team from scratch (difference 2.7 points [$-0.4$, 5.7];
49 against 34 discordant runs, exact McNemar $p=.12$). The repair needed a
median of 2{,}309 tokens instead of 4{,}167 (45\% fewer), and it preserved
79\% of the values that the team had already accepted, so work that had passed
its checks was not redone. Of the repairs, 72\% were applied in place, that is,
only the diagnosed rule or binding changed. Repair was never worse than
rebuilding for any model (Qwen2.5-Coder 7B: 16.9\% against 10.6\%; Devstral:
20.0\% against 18.4\%; Qwen2.5-Coder 32B: 27.9\% for both).

\paragraph{The price} Traceability is not free. Adaptive attribution uses 7.7
LLM calls per failure, against one call for the cheapest transcript method; for
Qwen2.5-Coder 32B, attribution takes a median of 793\,s in the runs that invoke
it, more than half of the median run (1{,}444\,s), because replays re-run
stochastic bindings. Repair also helps only a minority of failed runs: four in
five failures remain failures after one repair round.

\small\textbf{Answer to RQ2.} Yes. The trace locates the responsible binding
of 96--98\% of injected faults, against at most 62\% (step) for transcript
methods, and names the fault class correctly in 74--75\% of cases, against at
most 33\%. A checked repair of the diagnosed part is at least as successful as
rebuilding the team, uses 45\% fewer tokens and keeps 79\% of the accepted work.
\emph{Price:} about eight LLM calls per failure, which can double the time of a
failing run.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{RQ3: Are composition defects caught before the team runs?}\label{sec:res-prev}

\paragraph{Setup} We test the checker in three ways. \emph{Mutation of natural
teams:} we wrote nine mutation operators, one or two per defect class, and
applied each at every applicable site of the 3{,}379 teams that builders
generated and the checker admitted in the main runs, yielding 151{,}516 mutants
(Table~\ref{tab:natmut}). Each mutant was checked with three variants of
\W{4}: \emph{path-only} (some class-level path leads from each goal class to a
behaviour-checked class), \emph{one-sided} anchoring (goal data must reach the
check of a value), and the \emph{two-sided} anchoring of
Definition~\ref{def:anchor} (goal data must reach both the producer and the
check). \emph{Mutation of hand-written teams:} the same operators applied to the
typed \devteam{} and a requirements team (85 mutants), under two goal
declarations: $G_1$ declares only checked obligations, and $G_2$ also declares
the deliverables. \emph{Independently seeded defects:} to avoid testing the
checker only on defects designed for it, Gemma~3 was asked to seed one defect
into each of 300 admitted builder teams (60 per defect class), seeing only the
plain-language definitions of \D{1}--\D{5} (Table~\ref{tab:defects}) and never
\W{1}--\W{6}. When its edit left the team unchanged, it was told so and asked
again, up to four times; 23 teams that were still unchanged were removed, leaving
275 seeded teams (48--59 per class). Sixty clean teams served to measure false
alarms. Three LLM critics
(Qwen2.5-Coder 7B and 32B, Devstral) received the same JSON specification and
the same defect definitions.

\paragraph{(1) The checker rejects almost every mutant, but only with two-sided
anchoring} High detection of mutants is expected by construction: each
operator breaks a condition that the checker states. The analysis is
nonetheless informative in two ways. First, it shows that the conditions hold
on teams written by LLMs, not only on our own: with two-sided anchoring, the
checker rejects 151{,}496 of the 151{,}516 mutants (99.99\%). The 20 survivors
are 12 tool removals, 7 rule deletions and one detached team. Second, it
exposes a blind spot of the weaker formulations. The \emph{detach} operator
keeps every rule but removes all goal data from the producers' footprints, so
agents write code without ever seeing the task's examples. Path-only coverage
admits all 3{,}377 detached teams, and one-sided anchoring admits 98.6\% of
them, because the validators still read the examples. Only two-sided anchoring
rejects them. The hand-written teams add a second lesson: under $G_1$, deleting
the rule that creates the requirements team's API operations escapes all
variants, because every declared obligation still reaches a check; declaring
the deliverables ($G_2$) catches it, and two-sided anchoring then rejects all 85
mutants. Coverage is relative to what is declared.

\paragraph{(2) Independently seeded defects} With two-sided anchoring, the
checker rejects 173 of the 275 seeded teams (62.9\%, Wilson 95\% CI [57.1,
68.4]) and raises false alarms on 8 of the 60 clean teams (13.3\% [6.9, 24.2];
one-sided and path-only: 11.7\%). Detection depends on the defect class: 98\%
for claimed completion (\D{4}), 71\% for coverage gaps (\D{1}), 66\% for
conflicting writers (\D{3}), 47\% for missing capabilities (\D{5}) and 27\% for
hand-off mismatches (\D{2}). The three variants of \W{4} perform alike here,
because no seeded defect detaches the goal from the producers. The LLM critics
do not discriminate at all. The two larger critics (Qwen2.5-Coder 32B and
Devstral) report a defect in \emph{every} team, seeded or clean (100\% and
100\%), and the 7B critic reports one in 40.4\% of seeded and 40.0\% of clean
teams. A critic that flags every team, or flags teams at random, gives an
engineer no information about a specific team. The checker is the only one of
the four judges whose verdict separates defective from clean teams.

The 102 seeded defects that the checker misses show the limits of a structural
check. Reading their descriptions, we grouped them roughly as follows. About
40\% add an agent or a tool that no hand-off uses, which the seeder labelled as
a conflict (\D{3}) or a capability mismatch (\D{5}). Such an addition does not
break the team, so arguably it is not a composition defect at all. About 20\%
change a prompt's wording or a declared output format but no footprint, for
example asking for a return type ``as a string''; the checker reasons about
what an agent reads and writes, not about what its prompt says. About 15\%
remove a guard; whether a guard is needed is undecidable in general, and the
checker only warns about guards (Section~\ref{sec:app-check}). The rest change
behaviour that no static check can see, for example an agent instructed to
introduce off-by-one errors. The false alarms, in turn, come from clean teams
in which a producer legitimately does not read the goal.

growth exponents 1.53 and 1.50). Most of the time goes to \W{1} and \W{4}
(Figure~\ref{fig:scale-b}). Teams that builders actually generate have three to
six views. An \auto{} run performs a median of six checks, which take a median
of 3.0\,ms in total (95th percentile 7.2\,ms). In a median Qwen2.5-Coder 32B
ClassEval run of 1{,}444\,s, checking accounts for a share of $2\times10^{-6}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:scale-d}). The cost of \auto{} is the cost of its LLM calls,
not of its checks.

\paragraph{The price: weak builders often produce no team} A checker that
rejects defective teams also rejects the only teams that a weak builder can
write. Figure~\ref{fig:admission} shows a sharp divide. Qwen2.5-Coder 32B and
Devstral produce an admissible team in 96--97\% of sessions, nearly always at
the first proposal, and StarCoder2 in 80\%. Gemma~3, Qwen2.5-Coder 7B, Code Llama
and Granite-Code reach only 19--55\%. Across the 8{,}851 rejected proposals, the
first violation was \W{1} (a reference to a feature that the builder never
declared) in 73\%, \W{4} in 14\% and \W{2} in 12\%. These are mostly
inconsistencies within a single proposal, not poor design decisions. Diagnostic
rounds help little: they add 3--12 points of admission. Our local-revision
builder (\textsc{Build}, Section~\ref{sec:app-build}) did not improve on
whole-team revision for Qwen2.5-Coder 7B in an ablation on 40 ClassEval tasks
(21 against 28 admitted sessions; 7 against 14 discordant tasks). A refused
session is an honest outcome: the team does not run and does not claim success.
It is also lost work, and it is the main reason for \auto{}'s lower success
(Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}).

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, colback=modelbg, colframe=modelbar, boxrule=0.5pt, arc=1pt, left=6pt, right=6pt, top=3pt, bottom=3pt]
\small\textbf{Answer to RQ3.} Yes, for the defects it is designed for. With
two-sided anchoring, the checker rejects 99.99\% of 151{,}516 mutants of
LLM-written teams; path-only and one-sided coverage miss nearly all teams whose
producers never see the goal. On independently seeded defects, it is the only
judge whose verdict separates defective from clean teams: it catches 63\% of
them with 13\% false alarms, whereas the LLM critics flag clean and defective
teams alike.
A check takes milliseconds. \emph{Price:} for four of seven models, 45--81\% of
builder sessions never produce an admissible team.
\end{tcolorbox}

\paragraph{Success in the main study} Table~\ref{tab:success} reports success
per model. \auto{} succeeds less often than the single-agent baselines, with
mean success of 10.6\% (ClassEval) and 38.9\% (HumanEval+) against 24.5\% and
61.0\% for Single. The gap follows admission. For the two builders that produce
admissible teams in 96--97\% of sessions, \auto{} is on par with the multi-agent
baselines on HumanEval+ (Qwen2.5-Coder 32B: 83.3\% against 79.7--83.7\%;
Devstral: 77.0\% against 75.0--77.2\%; no significant difference), though below
a single agent on ClassEval. For the models whose builders often fail to produce
a team (Gemma~3, Qwen2.5-Coder 7B, Granite-Code, Code Llama), every refused
session is a failed task, and \auto{} is significantly below most baselines.
A compute-matched single agent with an execution gate (Single-Gate) has the
highest mean success on both benchmarks, in line with reports that the gains of
multi-agent systems over strong single agents are
small~\cite{cemri2025mast,kim2025scaling}.

\paragraph{A post-hoc revision} After seeing these results, we revised \auto{}
in three ways that target the lost work rather than the checker:
(R1) after admission, the runtime completes a deliverable specification that
the builder left incomplete (for example, a method without a binding), so that
admitted teams do not stall on an omission;
(R2) when no candidate passes all validators, the runtime keeps the candidate
that passes the most documented examples instead of none; and
(R3) when the builder's team is not admitted, \auto{} falls back to a typed
team from a library of admitted teams instead of refusing.
The checker, the anchoring criterion and the baselines are unchanged. On the
held-out tasks, the revision raised \auto{}'s success with Qwen2.5-Coder 7B from
9.6\% to 21.2\% (ClassEval) and from 28.8\% to 71.8\% (HumanEval+), and with
Qwen2.5-Coder 32B from 23.8\% to 30.0\% and from 81.9\% to 86.8\% (one sample
per binding; exploratory comparison).

The revision also shows the trade-off between success and reliability.
With Qwen2.5-Coder 32B, it kept the reliability advantage: silent failures
of 7.9\% (ClassEval) and 5.9\% (HumanEval+) against 28.8\% and 10.4\% for
Single-Gate on the same tasks. With Qwen2.5-Coder 7B on HumanEval+, it did not:
falling back to a library team and keeping best-effort candidates raised
silent failures from 5.9\% to 16.3\%, above Single-Gate's 13.5\%. Recovering
lost work can thus reintroduce the silent failures that the checker prevents,
when the builder is weak.

\paragraph{Experiment 1: does a larger budget close the gap?} A typed team
validates and repairs each method separately, whereas a single agent must get a
whole class right in one sample. We therefore expected \auto{}'s success to
rise with its budget faster than Single-Gate's, especially for large classes.
We pre-registered this experiment (hypotheses, design and analysis were written
down before any run; the protocol is in the package):
\emph{H1}: on ClassEval, the success of \auto{} rises more steeply with $\log_2 k$
than that of token-matched Single-Gate;
\emph{H2}: the difference is larger for classes with at least six methods than
for classes with at most three;
\emph{H3} (negative control): on HumanEval+ there is no such difference.
We varied the samples per binding $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, gave Single-Gate
\auto{}'s median output tokens at each level, used the revised \auto{}, and ran
on the held-out tasks with three seeds.

The hypotheses are not supported (Table~\ref{tab:exp1}). For Qwen2.5-Coder
7B, \auto{}'s success on ClassEval does not rise with the budget; it falls
slightly from 21.2\% to 17.5\% while its output tokens grow from 21.9k to 30.9k.
The condition~$\times$~$\log_2 k$ interaction is $-0.06$ (95\% CI $[-0.17,
0.04]$, $p=.24$; H1 not supported), and the three-way interaction with class
size is $0.09$ ($p=.40$; H2 not supported). Both conditions plateau: the change
from $k=4$ to $k=8$ is $-1.3$ points $[-5.4, 2.5]$ for \auto{} and $-2.5$
points $[-5.8, 0.4]$ for Single-Gate. On HumanEval+, as expected under H3, no
slope difference appears (Qwen2.5-Coder 7B: $0.04$, $p=.37$; Qwen2.5-Coder 32B:

reaches Single-Gate's level at $k=2$ and $k=4$ (88.5\% and 89.1\%), but not at

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What \auto{} offers: a team whose results can be trusted and
explained} Our results support two benefits and the mechanism behind them.
\emph{Reliability:} when an \auto{} team says done, it is rarely wrong, and when
it fails, it almost always says so (RQ1). \emph{Traceability:} when it fails,
the trace names the responsible binding and the kind of fault, and a repair of
that part is as effective as starting over at lower cost (RQ2). \emph{The
mechanism:} the checker rejects teams whose goal is not covered by executed
checks, and its rejection is what makes the ``done'' meaningful (RQ1, the
Typed-NC ablation) and the trace well formed (RQ3). Both benefits matter when
the cost of an undetected wrong result is high, for example when the output of
a team flows into a larger system without review, when several teams run
unattended, or when a failure must be explained to a person rather than merely
retried.

\paragraph{Why executing tests is not enough} One could expect any condition
that runs tests before declaring success to be as reliable as \auto{}.
Single-Gate refutes this: it executes the public examples and spends the same
tokens, yet it fails silently almost four times as often. The difference is coverage.
Single-Gate stops when the examples it happens to run pass; \auto{}'s $\varphi$
is true only when every declared obligation has been checked, and two-sided
anchoring ensures that the checks and the producers both see the goal. The
detach mutants of RQ3 show why the second side matters: without it, a team can
pass its checks while its producers never saw the task's examples.

\paragraph{What \auto{} does not offer} \auto{} does not make agents reason
better, and it does not raise task success over a single agent; it trades
success for reliability. The trade-off is clearest for weak builders, whose
sessions are refused, and in the post-hoc revision, where recovering refused
sessions reintroduced silent failures for the weakest model. We also found no
support for three further benefits that we had hoped for: more budget did not
help (Experiment 1); results were not more stable across seeds; and we cannot
say whether \auto{} reduces composition-caused failures, because LLM coders
did not agree on failure causes (Section~\ref{sec:rqs}).

\paragraph{When to use \auto{}} The results suggest a simple rule. If the goal
is the highest success rate per token, a single agent with an execution gate
is the better choice. If the goal is a result that can be trusted without
re-checking, and failures that can be located and repaired, \auto{} with a
capable builder offers both, at 7--16 times the tokens of a single agent. With a
weak builder, \auto{} remains honest, since refused sessions do not claim
success, but it does little useful work.

\paragraph{Builders are the bottleneck} Admission divides the models sharply,
and the most frequent first violation is a reference to a feature that the
builder never declared, an inconsistency within one proposal. Because the
checker's diagnostics are precise and cheap, they could serve as a training
signal for builders, which we have not explored.

\paragraph{Implications} \emph{Researchers} evaluating multi-agent systems
should report silent failures and the precision of ``done'' alongside success,
and should include a compute-matched single agent with an execution gate as a
baseline. \emph{Framework builders} could run \W{2}, \W{4} and \W{5} as a lint
over LangGraph or CrewAI teams, whose agents, typed state and edges map onto
views, hand-offs and write rights; we have not evaluated such a lint.
\emph{Practitioners} who need trustworthy results should prefer completion
signals that are computed from executed checks covering the goal over signals
that agents emit.

\paragraph{Future work} The revised \auto{} was evaluated on two models only.
Running it on all seven models and both benchmarks, and finding a recovery
strategy for refused sessions that does not reintroduce silent failures, is the
most direct next step. Others are builders trained on the checker's
diagnostics, validators whose adequacy is itself checked, and a human-coded
study of failure causes.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Construct validity} Each condition ends a run with its own signal,
so the reliability measures compare different mechanisms. This is intended: a
user sees exactly this signal. Silent failures and flagged failures can be
improved by a condition that rarely says done; we therefore report precision,
lift and the rate of ``done'' with them. Hidden tests are an imperfect
oracle~\cite{liu2023evalplus}, and a run that passes them may still be wrong.
Injected faults (RQ2) and mutants (RQ3) are synthetic. We mitigated this by
injecting into real runs, by mutating teams that LLMs wrote, and by having a
different model seed defects from plain-language definitions; the attribution
of natural failures remains untested.

\paragraph{Internal validity} The baselines are re-implementations of published
systems, and the runtime, checker, baselines and reference team come from the
same author. Typed conditions run on \tool{} and the others in a group-chat
harness with the same timeouts and sandbox. The revision of
Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} was designed after seeing the main results; we
evaluated it only on held-out tasks, did not change the baselines, and report it
separately. One model (Gemma~3) replaced a planned model for a technical reason,
not because of its results. LLM outputs are
non-deterministic~\cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism}; we use three seeds and
task-clustered intervals.

\paragraph{External validity} Results may depend on the three worked examples
given to builders, whose choice we did not vary; most admitted teams (71\%) reuse the
shape of one of the examples, although 207 distinct team shapes
appear. We study only Python tasks, which favour executable validators, and
open-weight models of up to 32B parameters; larger models may write admissible
teams more often, which would reduce the price of \auto{} but not necessarily
change its reliability. The transcript-based attribution methods were run with
a 32B judge, not with the proprietary models of their original papers.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity} We report task-clustered intervals and exact
paired tests, and Holm-correct the pairwise success comparisons. The
reliability differences of RQ1 are large compared with their intervals, except
for precision on ClassEval, where \auto{} and Single cannot be separated.
Experiment 1 was pre-registered; its exploratory comparison of the revised and
the original \auto{} was not.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\auto{} asks an LLM builder for a typed team of views and hybrid M2M
transformations, run by \tool{}, and admits the team only if a program can check
that its parts fit. In 44{,}352 runs with seven open-weight models, this made
LLM-built teams markedly more trustworthy: they had the fewest silent failures
of all conditions, flagged more than 90\% of their failures, and gave the most
informative ``done''. When they failed, their traces located the responsible
binding of 96--98\% of injected faults and named the fault class twice as often
as transcript-based methods, and checked repairs matched rebuilding at lower
cost. A millisecond check made this possible and rejected 99.99\% of 151{,}516
mutants of LLM-written teams. These benefits come at a price: lower success,
7--16 times the tokens of a single agent, and many refused sessions for weak
builders. \auto{} is therefore not a way to solve more tasks, but a way to know
which tasks were solved and why the others were not, keeping the principle:
LLMs propose; programs decide.
