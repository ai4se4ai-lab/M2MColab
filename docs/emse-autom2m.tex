\title[AutoM2M: Reliable and Traceable LLM-Assembled Agent Teams]{When the Team Writes Itself: Reliable and Traceable LLM-Assembled Agent Teams through Checked Model Transformations}

% \author*[1]{\fnm{Majid} \sur{Babaei}}\email{majid.babaei@ufv.ca}
% \affil*[1]{\orgdiv{School of Computing}, \orgname{University of the Fraser Valley}, \orgaddress{\state{BC}, \country{Canada}}}

\abstract{Team builders let a large language model (LLM) design a team of LLM
agents for each task. Such teams often report success when they have failed,
and their failures are hard to explain. The cause is a \emph{composition gap}:
agents are defined only by prose roles, hand-offs between them are free text,
and nothing checks that the parts fit together before the team runs.
We present \auto{}, which brings model-driven engineering to team building.
A builder LLM proposes a \emph{typed team}: each agent has a metamodel as its
interface, and each hand-off is a model-to-model transformation. Our runtime,
\tool{}, executes these hand-offs, with LLMs filling in only individual,
validated values. A checker admits a team only if six well-formedness rules
hold. Trace models make failures easy to locate, and every repair must pass the
same checker.
We evaluated \auto{} in 44{,}352 runs on ClassEval and HumanEval+ with seven
open-weight models under eight conditions, plus fault injection, independently
seeded defects and mutation analysis. \auto{} had the fewest silent failures
(about 6\%, against 18--20\% for a single agent and 27--56\% for other
multi-agent teams), partly through abstention; admitted teams alone still fail
silently about half as often as a single agent. Its traces located 96--98\% of
injected faults, and its checker caught 63\% of seeded defects in milliseconds,
which LLM critics could not do.
Checked interfaces do not make LLM-built teams solve more tasks, but they make
their claims of success more trustworthy and their failures traceable.}

\keywords{{LLM-based multi-agent systems, team builders, model-driven engineering, model-to-model transformation, ATL, static analysis, failure attribution, empirical study}}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Software is increasingly produced by \emph{teams} of large language model
(LLM) agents. Each agent is an LLM that runs in a loop with tools and a role,
and the team divides analysis, design, implementation and testing among its
members~\cite{he2025lma,liu2024agents4se}. The first such teams were designed
by hand~\cite{hong2024metagpt,qian2024chatdev,dong2024selfcollab}. A second
generation removes the human designer. \emph{Per-task team builders} read a
task, decide which agents to create, write their role prompts and wire who
talks to whom~\cite{song2024captain,chen2024autoagents,zhang2025metaagent,ke2025maszero};
offline methods search over agentic
workflows~\cite{hu2025adas,zhang2025aflow,zhuge2024gptswarm}; and orchestrators
trained by reinforcement learning pick the next agent, write workflows or emit
whole teams~\cite{dang2025puppeteer,nielsen2026conductor,ke2026masorchestra}.

These teams fail often, and their failures are hard to explain. Cemri et
al.\ annotated more than 1{,}600 traces of seven multi-agent frameworks and
found that system-design issues (44.2\% of failure-mode occurrences) and
inter-agent misalignment (32.3\%) outnumber task-verification problems
(23.5\%)~\cite{cemri2025mast}. Lin et al.\ locate errors on the \emph{edges} of
a team, the hand-offs, where information is dropped, corrupted or
re-interpreted~\cite{lin2026agentask}. Even naming the culprit is hard. On the
Who\&When benchmark of annotated multi-agent failures, the best automated
methods identify the responsible agent in 53.5\% of cases and the decisive step
in 14.2\%~\cite{zhang2025whowhen}. With full input traces, agent accuracy rises
only to about two thirds and step accuracy to about one
third~\cite{chen2026traceelephant}.

\paragraph{The composition gap} We hypothesise that part of these failures has
a cause familiar to every software engineer: the parts of the team have no
interfaces. A team builder composes a system out of components (agents) and
connectors (hand-offs). Yet each component is specified only by a
natural-language job description, every hand-off is free text that the next LLM
re-interprets, and nothing checks that the parts fit before the team runs. A
deterministic audit of the 126 automatically assembled teams of Who\&When
(Section~\ref{sec:defects}) illustrates the point: none of the 356 generated
role prompts names a teammate or states what its agent must produce, and no
team plan assigns a step to a named role. These specifications are not wrong by
the standards of their builder, which never promised such interfaces; they
simply leave nothing that a program could check. Software architecture calls this
situation \emph{architectural mismatch}: components that work alone fail
together because of unchecked assumptions about each
other~\cite{garlan1995mismatch,garlan2009mismatch}. Its classical remedies are
typed interfaces, contracts and specified
connectors~\cite{meyer1992dbc,allen1997connection}. We call the absence of such
interfaces in LLM agent teams the \emph{composition gap}. It gives rise to
\emph{composition defects}, such as parts of the task that no agent owns,
hand-offs whose content the receiver cannot use, artefacts with two writers,
completion that is merely claimed, and work routed to agents without the
needed tools. In current team builders, such defects are neither prevented
before execution, nor detected during it, nor attributable after it.

\paragraph{Key insight} Model-driven engineering (MDE) already offers checkable
interfaces for artefacts that are derived from one another. A metamodel states
what an artefact may contain, a model-to-model (M2M) transformation states rule
by rule how one artefact is derived from
another~\cite{czarnecki2006survey,mens2006taxonomy}, and static analysis can
reject an ill-typed or incomplete transformation before it
runs~\cite{cuadrado2017static}. We therefore treat each agent's output as a
model, each hand-off as a transformation, and the team as a network of
transformations. Because an LLM, not a person, now writes these metamodels and
transformations, the composition gap moves up one level: from the messages to
the \emph{team specification}, which must itself be checked.

\paragraph{This paper} We present \auto{} (\emph{automatic model-to-model
teams}), which applies one principle at two levels: \emph{LLMs propose; programs
decide}.

At the level of hand-offs, \auto{}'s execution layer \tool{} executes
\emph{hybrid} transformations: a deterministic engine fixes which objects exist
and how they are linked, and an LLM fills in only the values that no formula
can compute, each from a declared input and behind a validator.

At the level of the team, an LLM proposes a \emph{typed team}, and a
deterministic checker decides whether the team may run. The checker evaluates
six well-formedness rules, the \emph{admission rules} \W{1}--\W{6}, on the
specification alone; each rules out a composition defect or a part of one. We
call the builder's proposals and revisions, the checker's verdicts and the
checked repairs together the \emph{build--check--repair loop}.

When an admitted team fails, its \emph{trace models}, which record for every
value the rule, agent and inputs that produced it, locate the failed value; a
bounded replay of that value classifies the fault; and every repair must pass
the same checker before it takes effect. We use the rule language of ATL as
notation and as semantics; our implementation is a Python engine that
interprets these rules rather than an ATL/EMF tool chain
(Section~\ref{sec:impl}). We keep two names because the parts are separable:
\tool{} executes any typed team, whether written by an LLM or by a person, and
\auto{} is the approach that builds, checks and repairs teams on top of it.

\paragraph{What an engineer should get} Our goal is not primarily higher task
success. Two properties matter at least as much when a team's output is used
without a human re-checking every result. The first is \emph{reliability}: when
the team says it is done, the result should be correct, and when it fails, it
should say so. A failure that is reported as a success, a \emph{silent
failure}, is the costly case, because nobody looks at it. The second is
\emph{traceability}: when the team fails, an engineer should be able to find the
responsible part, understand what went wrong and repair that part without
starting over. Both properties are standard expectations of conventional
software, and both are missing from free-form agent teams, whose ``done'' is a
message that an agent chooses to send and whose only record is a chat
transcript.

\auto{} is designed to provide both. Its completion signal is computed by the
engine from executed checks that the checker has shown to cover the goal, and
its traces link every value to the rule, agent and inputs that produced it.
Both rest on the checker, which must catch composition defects before the team
runs. This machinery has a price in tokens, time and, for builders that cannot
write an admissible team, lost work. We therefore ask three research
questions, each about one benefit and its price:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \RQ{1} (Reliability): When an \auto{} team reports that it is done, can
we believe it, and when it fails, does it say so?
\item \RQ{2} (Traceability): When an \auto{} team fails, can the failure be
traced to the responsible part of the team, explained and repaired there?
\item \RQ{3} (Prevention): Does the admission check catch composition defects
before the team runs, and what does it cost?
\end{itemize}
We answer them with 44{,}352 runs of eight conditions on ClassEval and
HumanEval+ with seven open-weight models (RQ1), 1{,}108 injected faults
compared with four transcript-based attribution methods (RQ2), and 275
independently seeded defects compared with LLM critics, plus 151{,}516 mutants
of LLM-written teams (RQ3). We then report the price in task success, including a
pre-registered experiment that tested whether a larger budget closes the gap.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*}, leftmargin=2.2em, itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{A problem analysis} that grounds five composition defects
(\D{1}--\D{5}; the six admission rules cover them, with \D{2} split over two
rules) in published failure taxonomies and in an audit of 126
automatically built teams (Section~\ref{sec:defects}).
\item \emph{A formulation} of LLM agent teams as networks of hybrid M2M
transformations, built on standard MDE notions: the semantics of hybrid
hand-offs, the \tool{} runtime that executes them, and typed teams as their
checkable specifications (Section~\ref{sec:approach}).
\item \emph{Six admission rules}. Three of them (\W{1}--\W{3}) adapt
established static checks of model transformations to LLM-written teams; the
other three are new for agent teams, most notably a two-sided, feature-level
\emph{anchoring} criterion for coverage (\W{4}) and engine-decided completion
(\W{5}). We add two propositions on their cost and on the structural coverage
they guarantee, and an attribution procedure that turns failure localisation
into a lookup plus a bounded replay (Section~\ref{sec:approach}).
\item \emph{An empirical evaluation} of reliability, traceability and
prevention against single-agent, compute-matched single-agent, free-form, critic
and shared-schema baselines and two ablations, with fault injection,
independently seeded defects, mutation analysis and a pre-registered budget
experiment (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item \emph{A replication package} with the checker, the runtime, the team
fixtures, the analysis pipeline and all raw data (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Summary of findings}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Reliability.} \auto{} has the fewest silent failures of the eight
conditions on both benchmarks (5.9\% and 5.6\% of runs). Part of this is
abstention, but admitted teams alone still fail silently about half as often as
a single agent. On ClassEval, the repair rounds add more silent failures than
successes. A compute-matched single agent that runs the task's docstring
examples before stopping fails silently more often than a plain one. The price of
\auto{}'s caution is that more than half of its correct ClassEval results are
not reported as done.
\item \emph{Traceability.} The trace locates the responsible binding of
96--98\% of injected faults and names the fault class about twice as often as
the best transcript-based method. Repairing the diagnosed part is as successful
as rebuilding the team, with 45\% fewer tokens.
\item \emph{Prevention.} The checker is the only judge that separates
independently seeded defective teams from clean ones; only its two-sided
anchoring rejects teams whose producers never see the goal.
\item \emph{Price.} \auto{} uses 7--16 times the output tokens of a single agent
and succeeds less often (10.6\% against 24.5\% on ClassEval), because weak
builders often write no admissible team and typed teams lose success on
class-level tasks. A post-hoc revision recovers much of this loss on held-out
tasks; with a weak model, most of the recovery comes from falling back to a
hand-written team, which also brings back silent failures. A larger sampling
budget does not close the gap (pre-registered).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Running Example}\label{sec:background}

\auto{} sits between LLM-based multi-agent systems and model-driven
engineering. We therefore start on the agent side: what an agent team and a
team builder are (Section~\ref{sec:bg-agents}), a running example of a team
that a builder produces (Section~\ref{sec:example}), and the composition
defects such a team can contain (Section~\ref{sec:defects}). We then introduce
the MDE notions on which our remedy rests: models, metamodels and conformance
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-models}), and model-to-model transformations in ATL
(Section~\ref{sec:bg-m2m}).

\subsection{LLM agents, teams and team builders}\label{sec:bg-agents}

An LLM's output is sampled, so the same prompt can yield different answers,
some fluent yet wrong~\cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism,sallou2024breaking}. An
\emph{agent} runs an LLM in a loop with tools until the LLM declares that it is
done~\cite{wu2024autogen,yang2024sweagent}. To say precisely what a team
builder outputs, we need a minimal notion of a team.

\begin{definition}[Agent team, hand-off]\label{def:team}
An agent team is a tuple $(A,\rho,\kappa,E,\mathit{stop})$ of a set of agents
$A$, a role description $\rho(a)$ and a tool set $\kappa(a)$ for each agent
$a\in A$, a set of communication edges $E\subseteq A\times A$, and a
termination rule $\mathit{stop}$. A \emph{hand-off} is the passing of one
agent's output to another agent along an edge in $E$.
\end{definition}

In most frameworks, $\rho$ is free text, every hand-off is a natural-language
message, and $\mathit{stop}$ is either an agent's declaration (for example the
token \code{TERMINATE}) or a turn limit~\cite{wu2024autogen,song2024captain}.
A \emph{team builder} is a procedure, typically an LLM or a learned policy,
that maps a task to an agent team. Builders are optimised by end-to-end scores
or by an LLM's judgement of a transcript; Section~\ref{sec:rw-builders} reviews
them.

\subsection{Composition defects}\label{sec:defects}

Published failure analyses describe \emph{what} goes wrong in multi-agent runs.
To design checks, we need to know \emph{where in the team's specification} a
failure originates. We therefore read the published evidence as five defects
that a team builder can introduce when it composes agents, plus one
consequence.

\begin{definition}[Composition defect]\label{def:defect}
A composition defect is a property of an agent team's \emph{specification}:
which agent owns which work, what is handed over, which tools are where, and
how completion is decided. It is not a property of what one agent produces from
a given input. A specification has a composition defect if either
(a)~even agents that answer every request correctly cannot make the team
deliver its task, or (b)~the specification leaves the delivery of the task, or
the success of a hand-off, to an agent's unchecked judgement, so that a wrong
output of a fallible agent can pass through or end the run unnoticed.
\end{definition}

Part~(a) covers hard impossibilities, such as an unowned part of the task, a
reference the receiver cannot resolve or a required tool that no agent has.
Part~(b) covers missing safeguards: with ideal agents, a team whose
completion is a claimed \code{TERMINATE} (\D{4}), whose checks never read the
goal (\D{1}, check side) or whose artefacts have two writers (\D{3}) could
still deliver, but with the stochastic agents of practice nothing would detect
it when they do not. Section~\ref{sec:res-prev} shows that teams with defects
of kind~(b) can pass their tasks; such a defect is a hazard, not a guaranteed
failure.

Table~\ref{tab:defects} lists the five defects (\D{1}--\D{5}) and the
consequence C, together with the published failure modes that each subsumes.
The mapping from failure modes to defects is analytical; we do not estimate
how often each defect decides a failure (Section~\ref{sec:rqs} explains why),
but test whether the checker catches each defect (RQ3).
Single-agent reasoning errors (failure modes FM-1.1, 2.2 and 2.6 of the MAST
taxonomy~\cite{cemri2025mast}) are not composition
defects, because no composition can make an individual LLM reason correctly.

\subsection{Models, metamodels and conformance}\label{sec:bg-models}

Our remedy for the composition gap is to give every agent a precise interface.
MDE provides one: a metamodel, which states what a model may contain, and
conformance, which checks mechanically that a model stays within it. We use
metamodels as the ``forms'' that agents fill in, and conformance as the first
and cheapest check that what an agent produced is admissible.

MDE treats models, not code, as the primary artefacts of development and relies
on programs that check and transform
them~\cite{schmidt2006mde,brambilla2017mdse,naveed2024mde4ml}. A model
\emph{represents} an original for a purpose and keeps only the properties that
serve it; a metamodel is a model of the \emph{modelling language} in which such
models are written~\cite{kuhne2006matters}. A model \emph{conforms to} its
metamodel~\cite{bezivin2005unification}; this is a \emph{linguistic}
instance-of relation, between a model element and the language concept it
instantiates~\cite{kuhne2006matters,atkinson2003foundation}.
Definitions~\ref{def:metamodel} and~\ref{def:model} follow the usual reading of
metamodels as type graphs with constraints and of models as typed attributed
graphs~\cite{ehrig2006fundamentals}. Because each agent will own its own
metamodel, but all of them must refer back to the task, we also allow
references from one metamodel into another.

\begin{definition}[Metamodel]\label{def:metamodel}
A metamodel is a tuple $\MM=(C,\mathit{Att},\mathit{Ref},\preceq,\mathit{mult},\Phi)$
where $C$ is a finite set of classes; $\mathit{Att}$ a set of attributes,
each owned by a class $c\in C$ and typed by a primitive type; $\mathit{Ref}$ a
set of references $r:c\to c'$ between classes, some of which are
\emph{containments} (the target object is owned by the source object);
$\preceq$ a generalisation order on $C$; $\mathit{mult}$ a function that assigns
each attribute and reference a multiplicity $[l..u]$; and $\Phi$ a set of
well-formedness constraints in the Object Constraint Language
(OCL)~\cite{omg2014ocl}. A reference may also be \emph{external},
$r:c\to\MM'.c'$, typed by a class of another metamodel $\MM'$. A \emph{feature}
of $c$ is an attribute or reference whose source is $c$ or a superclass of $c$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Model, conformance]\label{def:model}
A model $M$ over $\MM$ is a finite object graph: a set of objects, each typed by
a class of $C$, with values (slots) for its features. $M$ \emph{conforms to}
$\MM$, written $M\conformsTo\MM$, iff every slot respects its feature's type
and multiplicity, every object has at most one container and containment has
no cycles, every constraint in $\Phi$ holds, and every external reference
points to an object of the right class in the referenced model.
$\Mods{\MM}=\{M\mid M\conformsTo\MM\}$ is the set of models of $\MM$.
\end{definition}

The symbol $\conformsTo$ for \emph{conforms to} follows the megamodel notation
of B\'ezivin and colleagues~\cite{bezivin2005unification}. For a metamodel
with external references, conformance is relative to the referenced models:
$M\conformsTo\MM$ is evaluated together with the models $M'$ of the
referenced metamodels $\MM'$, and $\Mods{\MM}$ is the set of models that
conform given those models. In \auto{}, every external reference points into
the goal view, so conformance of an agent's view is always evaluated relative
to the goal model $M_0$.

In the running example, the task of Listing~\ref{lst:task} is turned into a
model with one \cls{Task}, three \cls{Method} and three \cls{Example} objects.
Its metamodel, the \emph{goal view}, appears in Listing~\ref{lst:views}
(Section~\ref{sec:app-handoffs}), together with the views of the three agents.
The reference \code{MethodDesign.method : Goal.Method} in the Architect's view
is external: it points from a design object to the goal method it designs.

Conformance fixes what \emph{can} be stated, not whether the statement is
right. We call an agent team ``typed'' in this limited sense: its interfaces are
metamodels, so whether the parts fit together becomes a question about the
features that each part declares, reads and writes (made precise in
Section~\ref{sec:app-check}), rather than about prose. We do not use model
subtyping~\cite{steel2007typing}; every view is used exactly as declared.

\subsection{Model-to-model transformations and ATL}\label{sec:bg-m2m}

A hand-off between agents derives one artefact from others, for example a
design from requirements or code from a design. MDE describes such derivations
as model transformations, ``the heart and soul'' of
MDE~\cite{sendall2003heart}. A model transformation automatically produces a
model from one or more source models~\cite{hoppner2022mtl,czarnecki2006survey}.
It is \emph{exogenous} if source and target metamodels
differ~\cite{mens2006taxonomy}, and its \emph{intent} fixes the properties it
must satisfy, such as completeness~\cite{lucio2016intents}. In \auto{}, a
hand-off may read the outputs of several agents but writes into one agent's
view, which motivates the following restricted form.

\begin{definition}[M2M transformation]\label{def:m2m}
An M2M transformation is a relation
$T\subseteq\Mods{\MM_{s_1}}\times\dots\times\Mods{\MM_{s_k}}\times\Mods{\MM_t}$
between models of $k\ge1$ source metamodels and models of one target metamodel,
specified by a set of rules. It is \emph{functional} if every tuple of sources
is related to exactly one target.
\end{definition}

A hand-off in a software team is therefore an exogenous, unidirectional
transformation whose intent includes completeness: everything the sources state
must be reflected somewhere in the target. In the running example, the
Developer's hand-off \code{Design2Impl} reads the Architect's and the Tester's
views and writes the Developer's view.

We build on the rule language and execution model of
ATL~\cite{jouault2008atl,jouault2006transforming}, a transformation language
that combines declarative and imperative constructs, restricted to its
declarative core. ATL transformations are functional. The basic unit of an ATL
transformation is the matched rule.

\begin{definition}[Matched rule]\label{def:rule}
A matched rule is a tuple $r=(p_r,g_r,t_r,B_r)$ where the \emph{source pattern}
$p_r$ is a list of typed variables $x_i:C_i$ over source classes, the
\emph{guard} $g_r$ is a Boolean OCL expression over them, the \emph{target
pattern} $t_r$ is a list of typed variables over target classes, and the
\emph{bindings} $B_r$ are assignments $f\leftarrow e$ of an OCL expression $e$ to
a feature $f$ of a target variable. A \emph{match} of $r$ is an assignment of
source objects to $p_r$ that satisfies $g_r$.
\end{definition}

In the running example, the Tester's rule \code{Method2Test}
(Listing~\ref{lst:rules}) has the source pattern \code{m : Goal!Method}, no
guard, the target pattern \code{t : Test!TestCase}, and among its bindings
\code{name <- m.name}. Each goal method is a match, so the rule creates one
test case per method.

ATL executes matched rules in two phases~\cite{jouault2008atl}. The
\emph{matching} phase computes every match, allocates its target objects and
records a trace link. The \emph{initialisation} phase evaluates the bindings.
When a reference binding yields a source object, ATL \emph{resolves} it: the
target receives the object that was created from that source object, not the
source object itself. Sources are read-only and targets are write-only. ATL
also requires, and checks at run time, that each tuple of source objects is
matched by at most one rule, so the result does not depend on rule order.
Under this assumption, the target's \emph{structure} is a deterministic
function of the sources, and every target object is accounted for by a trace
link. Many errors, such as ill-typed navigation, unresolved bindings, rule
conflicts and missing mandatory features, can then be found
statically~\cite{cuadrado2017static}.

\begin{definition}[Trace link and trace model]\label{def:trace}
Let $\mathcal{R}_T$ be the rules of a transformation $T$ and
$\mathit{Match}_T$ the set of their matches in one execution. A trace link is a
triple $(m,t,r)$ of a match $m$, the set $t$ of target objects created for it,
and the rule $r\in\mathcal{R}_T$. The \emph{trace model}
$\TL_T\subseteq\mathit{Match}_T\times 2^{\mathit{Obj}_t}\times\mathcal{R}_T$, where $\mathit{Obj}_t$ is the set of objects of the target model, is
the set of trace links of that execution, persisted as a model in its own
right~\cite{jouault2005traceability,vara2014metagem}.
\end{definition}

ATL's trace links can be exported as models~\cite{jouault2005traceability} or
generated as first-class, queryable models~\cite{vara2014metagem}, and
incremental execution re-runs only the rules whose matches
changed~\cite{jouault2010incremental}. \auto{} relies on both: traces locate
failures, and incrementality lets the runtime recompute only values whose
inputs changed.

Two further notions name what \auto{} manipulates as a whole. A
\emph{megamodel} is a model whose elements are models, metamodels and
transformations~\cite{bezivin2005unification}; a typed team is one. A
\emph{higher-order transformation} takes or produces a transformation
model~\cite{tisi2009hot}; \auto{}'s compiler, which turns a typed team into
executable rule modules, is one in this sense. The name \tool{} abbreviates
\emph{Agent Hand-Off Transformations}.

\section{Approach}\label{sec:approach}

\auto{} applies one idea at two levels. At the level of hand-offs, its
execution layer \tool{} lets an LLM write individual \emph{values} while a
deterministic engine owns the \emph{structure} of each hand-off. At the level of
the team, \auto{} lets an LLM \emph{propose} a team while a deterministic
checker owns the decision to \emph{admit} it. Section~\ref{sec:app-overview}
gives an overview. The following sections introduce the parts in the order in
which each builds on the previous one: hybrid hand-offs, the language in which
teams are written (Section~\ref{sec:app-handoffs}); typed teams, which a builder
proposes (Section~\ref{sec:app-typed}); how an admitted team runs
(Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}); the admission check
(Section~\ref{sec:app-check}); how a builder uses that check
(Section~\ref{sec:app-build}); and how failures are attributed and repaired
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}). Implementation details are reported with the
evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the eight components of \auto{}. The compiler
and the runtime (\ding{176}, \ding{177}) form \tool{}, which executes a typed
team. The other components build, check, diagnose and repair the team. As in
any model-driven tool chain~\cite{bezivin2005unification,brambilla2017mdse},
the artefacts that flow between components are \emph{models} that conform to
metamodels (the goal model, the typed team, trace models and fault reports) and
\emph{transformations} (hand-offs and the rule modules compiled from them).
The compiler is a higher-order transformation~\cite{tisi2009hot}, since it
produces transformations, and so is a repair delta whenever it changes
hand-offs.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{172} Lifter.} Parses the task text into a \emph{goal
model}, which records the task's classes, methods and examples. This is a
deterministic text-to-model \emph{injection}, that is, parsing text into a
model~\cite{brambilla2017mdse,bruneliere2014modisco}.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{173} Builder.} The only component that designs. Its
\emph{Proposer} samples several candidate teams, each constrained to the
typed-team format (step~A), and the checker ranks them (step~B). Its
\emph{Reviser} then rewrites only those parts of the best candidate that the
checker's diagnostics name (step~C; Section~\ref{sec:app-build}).

\noindent\textbf{\ding{174} Typed team.} The builder's output is a model, not a
set of role prompts. It states the agents and their views, the hand-offs
between views, who may write what, which tools each agent has, what must be
covered and what is delivered, and when the run is done
(Section~\ref{sec:app-typed}). Because it is a model, a program can analyse it
before it runs~\cite{cuadrado2017static}.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{175} Checker.} Decides whether a team is admitted by six
admission rules, \W{1}--\W{6}, which cover typing, single writers, completeness,
coverage, completion and tools (step~B; Section~\ref{sec:app-check}). Its
diagnostics name the offending element, and their number ranks the builder's
candidates. A team that is still rejected after $k_{\mathit{adm}}$ revision
rounds is refused (\textsc{NotAdmitted}).

\noindent\textbf{\ding{176} Compiler.} Generates executable metamodels and rule
modules from an admitted team and loads the goal model (step~D). In MDE terms it
is a \emph{synthesis} higher-order transformation.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{177} Runtime.} Executes the hand-offs
(Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}). The engine creates all objects and links. For
each value that no formula can compute, it asks the LLM of the agent that owns
the target view, and a validator accepts or rejects the value. Agents never
converse with each other. The engine, not an agent, decides when the run is
done.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{178} Attribution.} When the run is not done (step~E),
the locator follows trace links to the value that failed, and a bounded replay
of that value decides why it failed (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}).

\noindent\textbf{\ding{179} Repair.} Chooses the cheapest repair for each
fault report. A \emph{mechanical} repair re-samples a value (a retry) and
leaves the team unchanged. Every other repair is a \emph{delta}, an edit of the
team model~\cite{kehrer2011lifting,tinnes2025evolution}: either an automatic
one, which widens a footprint by the paths that attribution found, or one
proposed by the builder's LLM. A delta takes effect only after the checker
admits the edited team (step~F).

\auto{} invents no new modelling concepts. It reinterprets an agent team in
established MDE terms and asks which classical guarantees survive when LLMs
write the transformations and produce their values. Table~\ref{tab:mapping}
summarizes this correspondence. Two findings from the MDE literature shaped the
design. First, developers and experts rank \emph{traceability} among the
transformation-language capabilities that matter
most~\cite{hoppner2024traceability,hoppner2022mtl}; \auto{} keeps traces as
first-class models and uses them not only for change propagation but also for
attribution. Second, model evolution is described by edit operations whose
effect can be expressed at the level of the modeller's intent and traced across
metamodel and model
layers~\cite{kehrer2011lifting,tinnes2025evolution,homolka2026coevolution};
\auto{}'s repair deltas are such operations on the team model.

entry point of \auto{}; the procedures it calls are detailed in the following
sections, and the letters in its comments are the steps of
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{AutoM2M}: build, admit, run, attribute and repair a team for task $x$}\label{alg:auto}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require task $x$; building budgets $k_{\mathit{prop}}, k_{\mathit{rev}}, k_{\mathit{adm}}$; repair rounds $k_{\mathit{rep}}$
\Ensure the deliverable, or a refusal, or fault reports
\State $M_0 \gets \textsc{Lift}(x)$ \Comment{goal model; deterministic}
\State $(\Team, \mathit{diag}) \gets \textsc{Build}(x, M_0)$ \Comment{steps A--C; Alg.~\ref{alg:build}}
\If{$\mathit{diag} \neq \emptyset$}
  \State \Return \textsc{NotAdmitted}$(\mathit{diag})$ \Comment{no team runs, nothing is claimed}
\EndIf
\For{$j = 0, 1, \dots, k_{\mathit{rep}}$}
  \State $(\mathit{done}, F) \gets \textsc{RunTeam}(\Team, M_0)$ \Comment{step D; Alg.~\ref{alg:handoff}}
  \If{$\mathit{done}$} \Return the deliverable assembled from $\Team$'s values \EndIf
  \If{$j = k_{\mathit{rep}}$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State $R \gets \{\textsc{Attribute}(f) \mid f \in F\}$ \Comment{step E; Alg.~\ref{alg:attr}}
  \State $(\mathit{retry}, \Delta) \gets \textsc{PlanRepair}(R)$ \Comment{mechanical repairs and a delta}
  \If{$\Delta \neq \emptyset$ \textbf{and} $\textsc{Admit}(\Team\oplus\Delta) = \emptyset$} \Comment{step F; Alg.~\ref{alg:admit}}
    \State $\Team \gets \textsc{Apply}(\Team, \Delta)$ \Comment{in place, by extension or rebuild}
  \EndIf
  \State clear the escalation marks of the values in $\mathit{retry}$ \Comment{fresh budget of $\ksamp$}
\EndFor
\State \Return \textsc{Failed}$(R)$ \Comment{fault reports of the last round}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{How to read Algorithm~\ref{alg:auto}.} Lines~1--2 turn the task into a
goal model and ask the builder for a team; \textsc{Build} returns the best team
it found together with the checker's diagnostics on it. If diagnostics remain
(lines~3--4), \auto{} refuses: the session ends without running anything, and
therefore without claiming success. Otherwise, the loop of lines~5--13 runs the
team at most $k_{\mathit{rep}}{+}1$ times. \textsc{RunTeam} (line~6) compiles the
team, executes its hand-offs to a fixpoint and evaluates the acceptance
predicate $\varphi$; it returns whether $\varphi$ holds and the set $F$ of
failures, each of which names a failed clause and, where possible, a value of
the team. If $\varphi$ holds, the run is done (line~7). Otherwise, every
failure is attributed (line~9) and a planner turns the fault reports into two
kinds of repair (line~10): \emph{mechanical} ones, which re-sample a value
without changing the team (a retry or the re-sampling of an upstream value), and
a \emph{delta} $\Delta$, which edits the team model (an automatic widening of a
footprint, or an edit proposed by the builder's LLM). Only the delta changes the
specification, so only the delta passes the checker (line~11); a delta that the
checker rejects is discarded and $\Team$ stays as it was. $\Team\oplus\Delta$
denotes the edited team; \textsc{Apply} (line~12) installs it in the running
team in one of three modes (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}). Line~13 clears the
escalation marks of the values to be retried, so that the next run samples
them again with a fresh budget, and if the last round still fails, the fault
reports are returned (line~14). In our implementation, line~9 attributes at
most two failures per round (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

All rounds of the loop share one \emph{session}: the compiled team, the
current models of all views, their trace models, stamps and escalation marks.
\textsc{RunTeam} compiles $\Team$ only when the session does not yet hold a
compiled form of it, and otherwise continues from the session's state; only a
rebuild (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}) starts a new session. A value therefore
keeps its stamp across rounds unless something makes it stale: a changed
source location, an in-place delta that changes its rule, prompt or
validator (which removes its stamp), or the re-sampling of an upstream value
(which removes that value's stamp, so that its dependants become stale through
their digests). A retry, in contrast, only clears an escalation mark. In the
evaluated implementation, it does not remove the stamp of a value that was
accepted and later fails $\mathit{valid}$; such a value is re-sampled only
if one of the other changes makes it stale (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).

\paragraph{The running example through Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}} The lifter
turns \code{TaskBoard} into a goal model with one task, three methods and three
examples. The builder's first proposal for \devteam{} has an Architect, a
Tester and a Developer, but contains one instance of each defect; the checker
rejects it with five diagnostics, and the reviser repairs the named parts until
the team is admitted (Section~\ref{sec:app-check}). The compiled team then runs.
The implementation of \code{mark\_done} fails, attribution traces the failure to
the Tester's input, and an automatic delta widens that input, after which the
engine declares the run done (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}).

\subsection{Hybrid hand-offs}\label{sec:app-handoffs}

This section defines the language in which every hand-off of a typed team is
written: views as the agents' interfaces, and hybrid rules that split each
hand-off between a deterministic engine and an LLM.

\paragraph{Views} Every agent owns a \emph{view}, a metamodel
(Definition~\ref{def:metamodel}) that fixes what the agent may state. Views stay
heterogeneous: each is sized to its agent's job, and how views correspond is
recorded by trace models (Definition~\ref{def:trace}) rather than by a shared
schema, as in view-based
development~\cite{klare2021vitruvius,bruneliere2019views}. Metamodels of this
kind are also used to define the views through which information about a system
is retrieved~\cite{kirschner2025retriever} and the inputs of model-based
analyses~\cite{cortellessa2022performance}. One view is special: the
\emph{goal view} $\MM_0$, into which the lifter turns the task. The lifter
separates examples from prose: every docstring example becomes an object of its
own rather than a part of the docstring text. Whether a hand-off reads the
examples is therefore visible in its data flow, which the coverage check of
Section~\ref{sec:app-check} relies on.

A rule that reads only \code{Method.docstring} does not see the examples.

\paragraph{Hybrid rules} A hand-off is written as a set of matched rules
(Definition~\ref{def:rule}). Some of its bindings can be computed by a formula,
such as copying a method's name; others, such as the body of a method, can only
be written by an LLM. \tool{} splits every rule along the line that ATL's
two-phase execution already draws: matching, object creation and reference
resolution stay with a deterministic engine, and only the bindings whose value
no OCL expression can compute are delegated to an LLM.

\begin{definition}[Hybrid rule, stochastic binding]\label{def:hybrid}
A hybrid rule is a matched rule whose bindings are partitioned as
$B_r=B_r^{\mathrm{str}}\uplus B_r^{\mathrm{sto}}$. A \emph{structural} binding
$f\leftarrow e\in B_r^{\mathrm{str}}$ is evaluated by the engine. A
\emph{stochastic} binding
$b=f\leftarrow\texttt{@llm}(\pi_b,\,e_b)\ \texttt{@check}\ \chk_b\in B_r^{\mathrm{sto}}$
targets a primitive attribute $f$; $\pi_b$ is a prompt, $e_b$ an OCL expression
over the rule's source variables, and $\chk_b$ a validator. A \emph{hybrid
hand-off} is a transformation whose rules are hybrid rules.
\end{definition}

Two design choices follow from the aim of making failures diagnosable. First,
each LLM call sees a declared input, its \emph{footprint}, rather than the
whole conversation. What a value was produced from is then known exactly and
can be recorded and replayed. (A re-sample after a rejection additionally shows
the LLM its own rejected value and the validator's message;
Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}.) Second, a validator may read data that the LLM
does not see; for example, a test validator may compare a test against the
goal's examples even when the LLM writes the test from the docstring. A version
stamp, finally, records the source inputs a value was produced and checked
from, so that the value can be recomputed when these inputs change. (A
behaviour validator also sees the other values of the deliverable, which the
stamp does not cover; Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime} explains how the run
handles this.)

\begin{definition}[Footprint, validator reads, version stamp]\label{def:footprint}
Let $b$ be a stochastic binding of rule $r$ and $m$ a match of $r$. A
\emph{location} is a pair (object, feature) of a source model. The
\emph{footprint} $\fp_b(m)$ is the set of locations that the expression $e_b$
navigates for $m$; they are the only values the LLM may see. The
\emph{validator reads} $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$ are the source locations that
$\chk_b$ inspects besides the candidate value. $\chk_b(v,\mathit{vr}_b(m),M_t)$
is a predicate over the candidate value $v$, the values at
$\mathit{vr}_b(m)$ and the target model $M_t$ into which $v$ is placed; a
form validator ignores $M_t$, whereas a behaviour validator executes $v$
inside the deliverable assembled from $M_t$, that is, together with the
sibling values of $M_t$. The \emph{version stamp} of an accepted value is the
digest $\#(\fp_b(m)\cup\mathit{vr}_b(m))$ of the values at all source
locations its production and its check read; it does not cover $M_t$.
\end{definition}

A value written by an LLM must not decide which objects exist; otherwise the
structure of a hand-off would itself become random. We therefore require
\emph{stratification}: no guard and no structural binding may read a feature
that a stochastic binding writes. This requirement is checked by \W{1}
(Section~\ref{sec:app-check}).

Our runtime departs from ATL in one respect. A reference binding whose expected
type lies in a \emph{source} view (for example \code{method <- m}, typed
\code{Goal.Method}) creates a cross-view link to the source object instead of
being resolved through the trace, because agents' views refer to the goal by
design. ATL would resolve \code{m} to the target that \code{m}'s own rule
created, which here has the wrong type; we apply that resolution only to
references whose type lies in the \emph{target} view. Two consequences make
this rule precise. First, resolution uses only the trace model of the
hand-off being executed, never those of earlier hand-offs: a binding whose
type lies in a source view $\MM_k$ must itself evaluate to an object of
$\MM_k$ (\W{3} checks this statically). Second, every rule of a typed team has
exactly one target variable, so a trace link always has one target object;
when a target-view reference yields a source object that occurs in several
matches of a rule with a multi-variable source pattern, the engine resolves
it to the target of the first such match in match order, and \W{3} only
checks that some rule maps the object's class to the expected class, not
that the resolution is unique. In addition, trace models are persisted, as in
MeTAGeM-Trace~\cite{vara2014metagem}.

We can now state what a hybrid hand-off means. Because its values are sampled,
a hand-off cannot denote a single target model. It denotes the set of targets
that its validators would accept.

\begin{definition}[Semantics of a hybrid hand-off]\label{def:hsem}
Let the \emph{structural part} $T^{\mathrm{str}}$ of a hybrid hand-off $T$ be
$T$ with every stochastic binding removed. Given source models $\vec M$, the
\emph{skeleton} $T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)$ is computed by ATL's matching and
initialisation, with the cross-view links above, and is deterministic. Like a
relation of QVT Relations (QVT-R)~\cite{omg2016qvt,stevens2010qvt}, and unlike
an ATL function, $T$ denotes the \emph{set} of admissible targets
\[\llbracket T\rrbracket(\vec M)=\{M_t\mid M_t\sqsupseteq T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)\ \wedge\ \forall(m,b):\ \chk_b(M_t[m,b],\mathit{vr}_b(m),M_t)\},\]
where $M_t\sqsupseteq T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)$ means that $M_t$ extends the
skeleton by exactly one value $M_t[m,b]$ for each match $m$ and stochastic
binding $b$. Because a behaviour validator reads the whole of $M_t$,
admissibility is a joint constraint on all values of $M_t$, not a conjunction
of independent constraints per value. We require that $T$'s target view is not one of its source views
($\mathit{tgt}(T)\notin\mathit{src}(T)$). An execution
of $T$ samples one element of this set, or \emph{escalates} the bindings for
which no admissible value was found. An escalated execution lies outside
$\llbracket T\rrbracket$ and makes the run fail; an escalation is a recorded
fault, not a hand-over to a person.
\end{definition}

During execution, each value is checked as soon as it is sampled, on the
partially filled $M_t$ in which values not yet accepted are replaced by stubs.
That a complete $M_t$ lies in $\llbracket T\rrbracket(\vec M)$ is decided only
at the end of the run, by the clause $\mathit{valid}$
(Definition~\ref{def:accept}), which re-evaluates every validator on the final
models.

A team of hand-offs denotes the set of tuples of models, one per view, in which
every hand-off's target is admissible for its sources. On an acyclic hand-off
graph, such tuples are obtained hand-off by hand-off in topological order. The
footprint does not appear in $\llbracket T\rrbracket$: it constrains how a
value is produced, not which values are admissible, and the runtime enforces it
(Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}).

\captionof{lstlisting}{The three hybrid hand-offs of the admitted typed \devteam{}. \code{@llm} marks a stochastic binding (second argument: footprint); \code{@check} its validator (Table~\ref{tab:validators}).}\label{lst:rules}
\begin{codebox}[language=ATL, style=model]
rule Method2Design {                             -- Goal -> Design (owner: Architect)
  from m : Goal!Method
  to   d : Design!MethodDesign ( name <- m.name, method <- m,

\subsection{What the builder proposes: typed teams}\label{sec:app-typed}

\tool{} executes a team but does not design one. In \auto{}, a builder LLM
writes the views, rules and validators, so they become an artefact that must be
checked. A free-form builder outputs role prompts
(Listing~\ref{lst:freeteam}); an \auto{} builder must output a \emph{typed
team}. Each component of a typed team makes explicit one aspect of the
specification that a composition defect concerns: the views and hand-offs fix
what is handed over (\D{2}), write rights fix who owns which artefact (\D{3}),
goal obligations fix what must be covered (\D{1}), the acceptance predicate
fixes how completion is decided (\D{4}), and the tool assignment fixes who can
do what (\D{5}).

\begin{definition}[Typed team]\label{def:typed}
A typed team is a tuple $\Team=(A,V,\mathbf{T},\omega,\kappa,G,\delta,\varphi)$
with the following components.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item $A$: a set of agents, each with a free-text role prompt.
\item $V=\{\MM_0,\dots,\MM_n\}$: a set of views with goal view $\MM_0$.
\item $\mathbf{T}$: a set of hybrid hand-offs, each with source views
$\mathit{src}(T)\subseteq V$ and one target view $\mathit{tgt}(T)\neq\MM_0$.
\item $\omega:A\to2^V$: the \emph{write rights} of each agent.
\item $\kappa:A\to2^{\mathit{Tools}}$: the tools of each agent.
\item $G$: a set of \emph{goal obligations} $(C,s,\mu,F_C)$. Each names a goal
class $C$, a \emph{scope} predicate $s$ that selects the objects of $C$ it
concerns, a mode $\mu$, and a set of \emph{anchor features} $F_C$ of $C$ (by
default, all attributes of $C$). Mode $\mathit{checked}$ requires that the
anchor features of every in-scope object reach a behavioural check; mode
$\mathit{delivered}$ requires a deliverable for every in-scope object.
\item $\delta=(D,g)$: the \emph{deliverable feature}, a target class $D$ and
its feature $g$ whose values form the deliverable.
\item $\varphi$: the proposed \emph{acceptance predicate}, a conjunction of
clauses that the engine evaluates at the end of a run
(Definition~\ref{def:accept}).
\end{itemize}
The goal view $\MM_0$ and the goal model $M_0$ are produced by the lifter; the
builder cannot change them.
\end{definition}

The \emph{owners} of a hand-off $T$ are the agents whose write rights include
its target view, $\mathit{owner}(T)=\{a\mid\mathit{tgt}(T)\in\omega(a)\}$; a
stochastic binding's owners are those of its hand-off. In an admitted team,
\W{2} makes the owner unique (Section~\ref{sec:app-check}).

\paragraph{Restricted expressions and the validator library} To keep every
admission rule decidable, a builder cannot write arbitrary OCL. Guards are
conjunctions of comparisons between navigation paths and literals; structural
bindings and footprints are navigation paths or literals; and validators are
chosen from a library rather than written. Each library entry declares a
\emph{strength}, either \emph{form} (the value parses, compiles or conforms) or
\emph{behaviour} (the value is executed against an oracle or a test); the tools
it needs; and which of its arguments it \emph{reads}. For a binding $b$, the
read arguments determine the validator reads $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$, and the
declared tools determine the tools $\tau(b)$ that the binding's owner needs;
$\tau(b)$ is derived, not proposed. Table~\ref{tab:validators} lists the
entries used in this paper.

\subsection{Running an admitted team}\label{sec:app-runtime}

Given an admitted typed team, the runtime executes its hand-offs, records what
every value was produced from, and decides at the end whether the run is done.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:handoff} shows both levels: \textsc{RunTeam}, which
Algorithm~\ref{alg:auto} calls, and \textsc{ExecuteHandOff}, which executes one
hand-off.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\tool{} runtime: running a team and executing one hybrid hand-off}\label{alg:handoff}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{RunTeam}{$\Team, M_0$}
  \State compile $\Team$ and load $M_0$, unless the session already holds them \Comment{state persists across rounds}
  \Repeat \Comment{at most $p_{\max}$ passes}
    \For{each hand-off $T$ of $\Team$ in topological order of the hand-off graph}
      \State $\mathit{Esc}_T \gets \textsc{ExecuteHandOff}(T)$
    \EndFor
  \Until{no model changed in this pass}
  \State \Return ($\varphi$ holds, the failed clauses and the values they name) \Comment{Def.~\ref{def:accept}}
\EndFunction
\Statex
\Function{ExecuteHandOff}{$T$} \Comment{sources $\vec M$, target $M_t$, trace model $\TL_T$}
  \State update the skeleton $T^{\mathrm{str}}(\vec M)$ in $M_t$ and $\TL_T$ \Comment{match, create, delete, link}
  \State $\mathit{Esc} \gets \{(t,b) \mid \mathit{esc}(t,b)$ equals the current digest of $b$ for $t\}$ \Comment{still open}
  \For{each trace link $(m, t, r) \in \TL_T$ and each stochastic binding $b$ of $r$}
    \State $d \gets \#(\fp_b(m) \cup \mathit{vr}_b(m))$ \Comment{digest of all that $b$ depends on}
    \If{$\mathit{stamp}(t, b) = d$ \textbf{or} $\mathit{esc}(t,b) = d$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf \Comment{fresh, or failed on this input}
    \State $\mathit{prompt} \gets$ role prompt of $\mathit{owner}(T)$, $\pi_b$, values at $\fp_b(m)$
    \State $\mathit{accepted} \gets \mathit{false}$
    \For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $\ksamp$ \textbf{while not} $\mathit{accepted}$}
      \State $v \gets \textsc{LLM}_{\mathit{owner}(T)}(\mathit{prompt})$
      \If{$\chk_b(v, \text{values at } \mathit{vr}_b(m))$}
        \State $t.f \gets v$;\ \ $\mathit{stamp}(t, b) \gets d$;\ \ $\mathit{accepted} \gets \mathit{true}$
      \Else
        \State append $v$ and the validator's message to $\mathit{prompt}$ \Comment{feedback}
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \If{\textbf{not} $\mathit{accepted}$} $\mathit{Esc} \gets \mathit{Esc} \cup \{(t, b)\}$;\ \ $\mathit{esc}(t,b) \gets d$ \EndIf \Comment{escalate}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathit{Esc}$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{How to read Algorithm~\ref{alg:handoff}.} \textsc{RunTeam} (lines~1--7)
executes the hand-offs in topological order, which exists because \W{5}
requires the hand-off graph to be acyclic, and repeats the pass until no model
changes, at most $p_{\max}$ times. In \textsc{ExecuteHandOff}, line~9 performs
ATL's matching and initialisation, made
incremental~\cite{jouault2010incremental}: it creates a target object for every
new match, deletes the objects of matches that disappeared, and evaluates all
structural bindings. This step is deterministic and calls no LLM.
Lines~11--22 then visit every stochastic binding of every match in match
order, which follows the order of the goal model (for methods, docstring
order). Every rule of a typed team has a single target variable, so each trace
link has one target object $t$, and we write $(t,b)$ for it and the binding
$b$ whose feature $f$ it fills. Line~13 skips a value whose stamp equals the
current digest of its footprint and validator reads, because nothing it
depends on has changed, and a binding that already escalated on that digest,
which line~10 keeps as an open escalation. Otherwise, the owner's LLM
is prompted with the footprint (line~14), and a sample is accepted as soon as
the validator accepts it (lines~18--19). After a rejection, the next prompt also
contains the rejected value and the validator's message (line~21), so that the
next sample differs. This feedback contains the LLM's own output and the
validator's verdict; a verdict may mention the example that was checked, which
is the only way in which validator reads reach the LLM. After $\ksamp$
rejections, the binding \emph{escalates} (line~22): it is recorded as a failure,
not handed to a person. The escalations of all hand-offs make the clause
$\mathit{noEsc}$ of $\varphi$ fail and are the starting points of attribution.

\paragraph{Sibling values} A behaviour validator executes a value together
with the other values of the deliverable (Definition~\ref{def:footprint}),
which the stamp does not cover. Two consequences follow, and the runtime does
not hide them. First, a value checked early in a pass is checked against stubs
of siblings that are accepted later; for example, if \code{mark\_done} were
implemented before \code{add\_task}, its examples would fail on the stub of
\code{add\_task}, and the value would escalate. Second, a value accepted
earlier keeps its stamp when a sibling changes later, although its verdict on
the final deliverable may differ. Repeating the pass does not resolve either
case: on an acyclic team executed in topological order, a second pass finds
every digest unchanged and therefore re-samples nothing, unless a request of
the first pass failed without a verdict (for example on a time-out), which
the pass then retries. Cross-sibling effects are instead caught at the end of
the run: the clause $\mathit{valid}$ re-checks every accepted value on the
final models, and the escalation of a value that failed only against a stub
is open in $\mathit{noEsc}$. Both go to the outer loop of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:auto}. For an escalation, attribution replays the value
inside the now complete deliverable, where it typically passes, and classifies
the fault as \textsc{Sampling}; the retry gives the binding a fresh budget in
the next round. For an accepted value that fails only $\mathit{valid}$, the
evaluated implementation is less complete: a retry does not remove its stamp,
so it is re-sampled only if a delta or an upstream re-sample makes it stale,
and otherwise the run fails with that value flagged, which is a lost success
but not a silent failure. Match order mitigates the first case, because methods are visited in
docstring order, in which set-up methods such as \code{add\_task} usually
come first. The repeated pass matters mainly for unchecked teams, which may
contain cycles (Section~\ref{sec:design}).

\paragraph{Agents at run time} An agent is a role prompt, a model and a declared
set of tools $\kappa(a)$. Agents do not converse. Each stochastic binding is one
call to its owner's LLM, whose prompt consists of the role prompt, $\pi_b$ and
the footprint; producing a value uses no tools. Tools are needed only by
validators, for example to execute code. Agents therefore see each other's work
only through footprints, which is what makes a recorded footprint the input of a
replay. The tool sets $\kappa$ are a declared part of the specification, which
\W{6} checks statically (Section~\ref{sec:app-check}).

\paragraph{Incremental re-execution} When the team or its inputs change, only
the affected values need to be produced again. Let a change $\Delta$ modify the
locations $\mathit{loc}(\Delta)$. A pair $(t,b)$ of a target object and a
stochastic binding becomes \emph{stale} if its footprint or validator reads
contain a location in $\mathit{loc}(\Delta)$, or a location written by a pair
that is already stale. The \emph{stale set} $\Stale(\Delta)$ is the least set of
pairs, over the trace models of all hand-offs, that is closed under these two
rules. All other values keep their stamps, so re-sampling after $\Delta$ costs
at most $\ksamp\cdot|\Stale(\Delta)|$ LLM calls per run. The bound covers only
the values in $\Stale(\Delta)$. Because stamps do not cover sibling values, a
change of one method can invalidate a sibling that is not in
$\Stale(\Delta)$; $\mathit{valid}$ then detects this at the end of the run,
and re-sampling the sibling needs a further repair round. The bound therefore
limits the cost of one run after $\Delta$, not the cost of re-establishing
$\varphi$. This is an analytical property of the runtime; we use it for repair
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}) but do not evaluate arbitrary source changes in
this paper.

In the running example, if E2.2 is changed to expect a \code{KeyError}, the
stale set contains the test code of \code{mark\_done} and, one step later, its
implementation; all other values keep their stamps.

\paragraph{Deciding completion} Defect \D{4} arises when ``done'' is a claim.
In \auto{}, the engine instead decides completion from the trace. To state that
a goal object has been covered, we need a notion of what was derived from it,
including objects that were only read through a footprint. Run records provide
it.

\begin{definition}[Run record, descendant]\label{def:record}
At run time, every trace link $(m,t,r)$ carries, for each stochastic binding
$b$ of $r$, a \emph{run record} $(b,\fp_b(m),\mathit{vr}_b(m),\mathit{stamp},v)$
with the accepted value $v$ or an escalation mark. The record stores the
\emph{values} at the locations of $\fp_b(m)$ and $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$ as they
were when $v$ was produced, not only the locations. Attribution
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}) replays a binding on its footprint evaluated on
the final models; these values equal the recorded ones unless a location was
overwritten after $v$ was accepted, in which case the stamp no longer matches
and $\mathit{fresh}$ reports it. The link \emph{connects} to
each object in $t$ every object of $m$ and every object that owns a location in
a recorded footprint or validator read. An object $o'$ is a \emph{descendant}
of $o$ if a chain of connections leads from $o$ to $o'$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Engine clauses, acceptance]\label{def:accept}
The \emph{engine clauses} are evaluated on the final models and run records:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item $\mathit{cover}(G)$ holds if every goal object $o$ in the scope of an
obligation $(C,s,\mu,F_C)$ has a descendant that holds an accepted value of a
behaviour-validated binding (if $\mu=\mathit{checked}$) or of the deliverable
feature (if $\mu=\mathit{delivered}$);
\item $\mathit{valid}$ holds if every accepted value passes its validator on the
final models;
\item $\mathit{fresh}$ holds if every stamp equals the digest of the current
values; and
\item $\mathit{noEsc}$ holds if no binding is escalated.
\end{itemize}
The acceptance predicate of an admitted team consists of all four engine
clauses and, optionally, \emph{library clauses} that the engine evaluates on the
deliverable, such as running its public examples (\W{5}). A run is
\emph{done} iff $\varphi$ holds.
\end{definition}

The clause $\mathit{cover}(G)$ is deliberately one-sided: it asks only that a
checked value descends from the goal object. That both the producer and the
check of that value read the goal is ensured statically, by \W{4}.

In the running example, each \cls{Example} has as descendant the test case
whose footprint read it, although no rule matches \cls{Example}. Because no
agent output can end the run, a Tester whose test for E2.2 fails cannot cause
the team to be declared done.

\subsection{Admission: six well-formedness rules}\label{sec:app-check}

The checker decides whether a typed team may run. It evaluates six \emph{admission rules}
on the typed team alone, without task data, LLM calls or execution.
\W{1}--\W{3} adapt to LLM-authored teams the checks that static analysers
perform on hand-written ATL transformations~\cite{cuadrado2017static};
\W{4}--\W{6} concern the team as a whole. Two graphs support them. The
\emph{hand-off graph} has an edge $\MM_i\to\MM_j$ for every hand-off with
$\MM_i\in\mathit{src}(T)$ and $\MM_j=\mathit{tgt}(T)$. The second graph tracks
data flow between features and is needed for coverage, as we explain first.

\paragraph{Why coverage needs feature-level data flow} The natural way to check
coverage is at the level of classes: require a path in the hand-off rules from
each goal class to some behaviour-checked class. Such a \emph{path-only} check
is not enough. A builder can create one test case per method, written from the
method's \emph{name} only. A path from \cls{Method} to a checked class then
exists, yet no example ever influences a check. Coverage must therefore be
checked at the level of features, and it must consider two roles that goal
data can play: input to the producer of a value, and input to that value's
check.

\begin{definition}[Feature-level data flow]\label{def:dataflow}
The data-flow graph $\mathcal{F}_\Team$ has a node $(C,f)$ for every class $C$
and feature $f$ of every view. A binding that writes feature $g$ of class $D$
contributes a \emph{production} edge $(C,f)\to_p(D,g)$ for every $(C,f)$ that
its expression or footprint reads, and a \emph{check} edge $(C,f)\to_c(D,g)$
for every $(C,f)$ that its validator reads. Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the set of
features written by stochastic bindings whose validator has behavioural
strength.
\end{definition}

A production edge transmits data: the producer sees it. A check edge transmits
nothing; it only gates whether the value is accepted. For a goal to be really
covered, the producer of a checked value must have seen the goal, \emph{and}
its check must test against the goal.

\begin{definition}[Anchoring]\label{def:anchor}
Write $\to_p^*$ for a path of zero or more production edges. A goal obligation
$(C,s,\mathit{checked},F_C)$ is \emph{anchored} at $(D,g)\in\mathcal{B}$ if,
for every anchor feature $f\in F_C$ (all anchor features must reach the same
$(D,g)$, not each its own),
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{production side:} $(C,f)\to_p^*\,\to_p(D,g)$, and
\item \emph{check side:} $(C,f)\to_p^*(C',f')\to_c(D,g)$ for some node $(C',f')$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In the running example, the path \code{m.examples.call} in the footprint of
\code{Method2Test} reads both \code{Method.examples} and \code{Example.call}, so
it contributes production edges from both to \code{TestCase.code}; and
\code{test\_valid} reads \code{m.examples.*}, which contributes check edges.
Figure~\ref{fig:w4} shows the resulting graphs for the admitted team and for the
first proposal. The \emph{one-sided} variant that requires only the check side
(ii) is weaker: Section~\ref{sec:res-prev} shows that it admits teams whose
producers never see the goal.

\paragraph{The six admission rules} Each rule below names the defect it
targets, states what it requires and, where useful, notes its limits. \D{2}
is split between two rules: \W{1} rules out its formal part (types and
paths) and \W{3} its content part (what a target must contain).

\paragraph{\W{1} Hand-offs are well typed and stratified (against the formal part of \D{2})}
Every rule reads only its source views and the goal view, which every agent may
read because agents' views refer to the goal by design, and writes only
features that its target class declares. Every guard, binding and footprint path
type-checks. Every stochastic binding targets a primitive attribute, has a
non-empty footprint and uses a library validator with correctly typed
arguments. Finally, the team is stratified (Section~\ref{sec:app-handoffs}).

\paragraph{\W{2} Every artefact has one writer (against \D{3})} The write
rights $\omega(a)$ are pairwise disjoint and together cover
$V\setminus\{\MM_0\}$, so $\mathit{owner}(T)$ is a single agent; and every
class is created by at most one rule, so no object has two producers. This is
stronger than ATL's requirement that each source tuple be matched by at most
one rule. The rule is conservative: rules with provably disjoint guards
could safely share a target class.

\paragraph{\W{3} Targets are complete (against the content part of \D{2})}
Every mandatory feature of a created class is bound exactly once. Every
reference binding whose type lies in the target view is \emph{resolvable}: some
rule creates an object of the expected class from the class of the source
object that the binding yields. Every reference binding whose type lies in a
source view yields objects of that type.

\paragraph{\W{4} Every goal is anchored (against \D{1})} \W{4} has four parts.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=2em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item Every obligation of mode \emph{checked} is anchored at some feature of
$\mathcal{B}$ (Definition~\ref{def:anchor}).
\item Every source-pattern class of a rule on an anchoring path must be
\emph{total}, inductively along its creation chain: a class is total if it is
a goal class, or if it is created by a rule all of whose source-pattern
classes are total. A class that is not total is a violation. Totality ensures
that the rule's other source variables can be filled for every in-scope goal
object. In addition, every rule on the chain should have a guard that is
implied by the obligation's scope. Because implication between guards is
undecidable in general, the checker tests it syntactically (the guard is
\code{true}, equals the scope, or is a \emph{join}, defined below) and
\emph{warns} about every other guard instead of rejecting the team.
\item Every obligation of mode \emph{delivered} reaches the deliverable feature
$\delta$: at least one of its anchor features has a path of production edges to
$\delta$.
\item Every view is reachable from $\MM_0$ in the hand-off graph and reaches a
view that contains a feature of $\mathcal{B}$, so no agent is idle and no
agent's work goes unverified.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{\W{5} The engine decides completion (against \D{4})} $\varphi$
contains all four engine clauses of Definition~\ref{def:accept} and, apart from
them, only library clauses; every clause is evaluated by the engine, and no
clause may be an agent's claim. In addition, the hand-off graph is acyclic, so
that hand-offs can be executed in topological order
(Definition~\ref{def:hsem}).

\paragraph{\W{6} Work goes to agents that can do it (against \D{5})} For every
stochastic binding $b$, $\tau(b)\subseteq\kappa(a)$ for its owner $a$, where
$\tau(b)$ comprises the tools that $b$'s validators need and any tools the
binding declares. The rule mirrors Gaia's permissions, under which a role cannot
act outside what it is granted~\cite{wooldridge2000gaia}: an agent that is not
declared to execute code must not own a value whose acceptance depends on
executing code. When \W{2} fails and a view has several writers, \W{6} is
evaluated for each of them.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Admit}: deciding whether a typed team $\Team$ may run}\label{alg:admit}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require typed team $\Team=(A,V,\mathbf{T},\omega,\kappa,G,\delta,\varphi)$; validator library
\Ensure the set $\mathit{diag}$ of diagnostics; $\Team$ is admitted iff $\mathit{diag}=\emptyset$; warnings are reported separately and never reject
\State $\mathit{diag} \gets \textsc{TypeErrors}(\mathbf{T}, V) \cup \textsc{StratificationErrors}(\mathbf{T})$ \Comment{\W{1}}
\State $\mathit{diag} \gets \mathit{diag} \cup \textsc{SharedWriters}(\omega, \mathbf{T})$ \Comment{\W{2}}
\State $\mathit{diag} \gets \mathit{diag} \cup \textsc{IncompleteTargets}(\mathbf{T})$ \Comment{\W{3}}
\State $(\mathcal{F}_\Team, \mathcal{B}) \gets$ feature-level data-flow graph and behaviour-checked features \Comment{Def.~\ref{def:dataflow}}
\For{each obligation $o = (C, s, \mu, F_C) \in G$} \Comment{\W{4}(a)--(c)}
  \If{$\mu = \mathit{checked}$ \textbf{and} no $(D,g) \in \mathcal{B}$ satisfies $\textsc{Anchored}(o, (D,g))$}
    \State $\mathit{diag} \gets \mathit{diag} \cup \{(\W{4}, o, \text{``not anchored''})\}$
  \EndIf
  \If{$\mu = \mathit{delivered}$ \textbf{and} no $f \in F_C$ has a production path to $\delta$}
    \State $\mathit{diag} \gets \mathit{diag} \cup \{(\W{4}, o, \text{``does not reach the deliverable''})\}$
  \EndIf
  \State add to $\mathit{diag}$ every non-total source class on an anchoring path of $o$; warn about every guard there that is neither implied by $s$ nor a join
\EndFor
\For{each view $V' \in V\setminus\{\MM_0\}$} \Comment{\W{4}(d)}
  \If{$V'$ is unreachable from $\MM_0$ \textbf{or} reaches no feature of $\mathcal{B}$}
    \State $\mathit{diag} \gets \mathit{diag} \cup \{(\W{4}, V', \text{``idle or unverified view''})\}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $\mathit{diag} \gets \mathit{diag} \cup \textsc{NonEngineClauses}(\varphi) \cup \textsc{Cycles}(\mathbf{T})$ \Comment{\W{5}}
\For{each stochastic binding $b$ and each writer $a$ of its target view} \Comment{\W{6}}
  \If{$\tau(b) \not\subseteq \kappa(a)$} $\mathit{diag} \gets \mathit{diag} \cup \{(\W{6}, b, a)\}$ \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathit{diag}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{How to read Algorithm~\ref{alg:admit}.} The checker evaluates all six
rules and never stops at the first violation, so that a single call reports
every problem of a proposal. Lines~1--3 are local checks of rules, write rights
and target classes. Line~4 builds the data-flow graph of
Definition~\ref{def:dataflow}, on which the coverage checks of lines~5--13
operate. For an obligation of mode \emph{checked}, line~6 searches for a
behaviour-checked feature at which it is anchored, that is, which every anchor
feature reaches both through production edges and through a check edge
(Definition~\ref{def:anchor}; Figure~\ref{fig:w4}); this is a reachability
question on $\mathcal{F}_\Team$. Line~10 rejects anchoring paths through
classes that are not total (\W{4}(b)) and records the guards that the checker
cannot prove harmless as warnings, which are returned alongside
$\mathit{diag}$ but do not prevent admission. Lines~11--13 reject idle views and views
whose work is never verified. Line~14 checks completion and acyclicity, and
lines~15--16 compare the tools a binding needs with those of every agent that
may write its target. Each diagnostic names the violated rule, the
offending element (a rule, a path, a write right or a clause) and a hint. Unlike
an LLM critic's comments, the diagnostics report \emph{every} violation of the
admission rules, and they point to a concrete element rather than advising
the builder to ``improve coordination''.

\paragraph{Properties} The checker runs on every candidate inside the builder's
loop (Section~\ref{sec:app-build}), so its cost matters.

\begin{proposition}[Cost]\label{prop:cost}
\W{1}--\W{6} are decidable on $\Team$ in time polynomial in $|\Team|$
(classes, features, rules, bindings and path segments).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
\W{1} navigates each path once; \W{2}, \W{3} and \W{6} compare finite sets.
\W{4}(a) and (c) are reachability problems over $\mathcal{F}_\Team$, whose size
has at most one node per feature and one edge per path segment of each
binding, so a breadth-first search from each anchor feature costs
$O(|\mathcal{F}_\Team|)$; implication between guards and scopes is tested
syntactically, in constant time per guard. \W{4}(d) and the cycle test of \W{5} are searches on the hand-off
graph.
\end{proof}

A second property explains why admission makes failures easier to locate. We
would like a failed $\mathit{cover}(G)$ clause never to stem from missing
structure, so that it always points at a value an LLM was asked to produce.
The property needs one more notion. A guard that equates two structural
references typed by the same goal class is a \emph{join}; in the running
example, \code{t.method = d.method} in \code{Design2Impl} is a join.

Because $\mathcal{F}_\Team$ is a graph of classes and features, not of
objects, the property also needs three assumptions about the goal model and
the structural bindings, which the checker does not verify:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(A\arabic*), leftmargin=2.4em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Non-empty navigation.} Every reference path that a rule on an
anchoring path navigates from its source variables to the class of the next
node yields, for each in-scope descendant of $o$, at least one object, and
the objects it yields include the descendant of $o$ (the path is surjective
onto in-scope objects). Containment paths of the goal view, such as
\code{Method.examples}, are surjective onto their contained objects, but an
optional reference may be empty: \code{export\_csv} has no examples, so an
obligation on \cls{Method} whose only anchoring path runs through
\code{Method.examples} has no descendant along that path for
\code{export\_csv}.
\item \emph{Inhabited source classes.} For every in-scope $o$, every other
source variable of a rule on the chain can be filled by an object that the
rule's guard relates to $o$; for a goal class, this requires the goal model
to contain such an object.
\item \emph{Identity-preserving joins.} The two sides of every join on the
chain are set by structural bindings that copy, along their creation chains,
a reference to the same goal variable, so that for the descendants of $o$
both sides point to the same goal object.
\end{enumerate}
All three hold for the teams of the running example, and (A3) holds whenever
both sides are copies of the goal object matched by the creating rules, as in
\code{method <- m}.

\begin{proposition}[Structural coverage]\label{prop:struct}
Let $\Team$ be admitted, let every guard of the rules on the paths that
anchor an obligation $(C,s,\mathit{checked},F_C)$ either be implied by $s$ or
be a join, and let (A1)--(A3) hold. Then, for every object $o$ of class $C$ that satisfies $s$, the
skeletons $T^{\mathrm{str}}$ create a chain of descendants of $o$ that ends in
a target object $t$ with a behaviour-checked feature, whose footprint and validator
reads both contain data derived from $o$. Consequently, if
$\mathit{cover}(G)$ fails for $o$ at the end of a run, some stochastic binding
on that chain is escalated or invalid.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Sketch]
By \W{1} (stratification), skeletons do not depend on LLM-written values, so
matches depend only on goal data and on objects created by earlier skeletons.
We proceed by induction along an anchoring path of
Definition~\ref{def:anchor}. \emph{Hypothesis:} after the skeletons of the
first $i$ rules on the path have run, an object of the class of the $i$-th node
exists and is a descendant of $o$. \emph{Step:} the $(i{+}1)$-th edge stems
from a rule whose source pattern contains that object, or an object from which
the rule's footprint navigates to it. (1)~This source variable is filled by the
hypothesis, and by (A1) the navigation reaches the object. (2)~The other
source variables are filled because their classes are total (\W{4}(b),
inductively along their creation chains) and, by (A2), inhabited. (3)~The
rule's guard holds, either because $s$ implies it or because it is a join
whose two sides, by (A3), point to the same goal object. (4)~The rule therefore fires and, by \W{3}, creates its
target with all mandatory features. Hence every object on the chain exists, and
only stochastic values can be missing. By Algorithm~\ref{alg:handoff}, a
missing or rejected value is escalated, or reported by $\mathit{valid}$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:struct} turns locating the symptom of a coverage failure
into a lookup: such a failure is never a silent structural gap but points at a
named binding. Its premise on guards is exactly what the checker verifies
syntactically in \W{4}(b); for a team admitted with a guard warning, or a
goal model or team that violates (A1)--(A3), the proposition does not apply,
and $\mathit{cover}(G)$ still detects the gap at run time, although without
naming a binding (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr} reports such failures as
\textsc{Coverage}).

\paragraph{Limits} Whether a guard excludes in-scope objects is undecidable in
general. The checker accepts guards that are implied by the obligation's scope,
warns about all others, and $\mathit{cover}(G)$ enforces coverage at run time.
Coverage is also relative to what is declared: an obligation that the builder
does not declare is not checked (Section~\ref{sec:res-prev} shows the
consequence for deliverables). Admission does \emph{not} guarantee that the
views capture what the task needs, that validators are strong enough, or that
agents reason well.

\subsection{Building a team under the checker}\label{sec:app-build}

The builder is the only component that designs, and it is an LLM. Its proposals
are imperfect: in our study, the most frequent first violation of a rejected
proposal is a reference to a feature that the builder never declared
(Section~\ref{sec:res-prev}).
\textsc{Build} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:build}) therefore treats the checker as an
oracle that the LLM consults, but never overrules: the LLM proposes and
revises, and only Algorithm~\ref{alg:admit} admits.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Build}: proposing and revising a typed team under the checker}\label{alg:build}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require task $x$; goal model $M_0$; budgets $k_{\mathit{prop}}$ (proposals), $k_{\mathit{rev}}$ (revision samples per round), $k_{\mathit{adm}}$ (rounds)
\Ensure a team $\Team$ and its diagnostics $\mathit{diag}$ ($\mathit{diag}=\emptyset$ iff $\Team$ is admitted)
\LineComment{Phase 1 (steps A and B): sample candidates and let the checker rank them}
\State $\mathcal{C} \gets \emptyset$
\For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $k_{\mathit{prop}}$}
  \State $\Team_i \gets \textsc{Propose}(x, M_0)$ \Comment{decoded under the typed-team schema}
  \State $\mathcal{C} \gets \mathcal{C} \cup \{(\Team_i, \textsc{Admit}(\Team_i))\}$
  \If{$\textsc{Admit}(\Team_i) = \emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf \Comment{admitted: stop sampling}
\EndFor
\State $(\Team, \mathit{diag}) \gets$ the candidate in $\mathcal{C}$ with the fewest diagnostics
\LineComment{Phase 2 (step C): revise only the parts that the diagnostics name}
\For{$\mathit{round} = 1$ \textbf{to} $k_{\mathit{adm}}$ \textbf{while} $\mathit{diag} \neq \emptyset$}
  \State $P \gets \textsc{Fragments}(\Team, \mathit{diag})$ \Comment{rules, views, write rights, \ldots{} that $\mathit{diag}$ names}
  \State $\Pi \gets \textsc{TypedPaths}(\Team, P)$ \Comment{paths that type-check from each rule's variables}
  \State $\mathcal{R} \gets \{\, \Team \lhd \textsc{ReviseFragments}_i(\Team, \mathit{diag}, P, \Pi) \mid i = 1..k_{\mathit{rev}} \,\}$ \Comment{$k_{\mathit{rev}}$ independent samples}
  \If{no $\Team' \in \mathcal{R}$ has fewer diagnostics than $\Team$}
    \State $\mathcal{R} \gets \mathcal{R} \cup \{\textsc{ReviseWhole}(\Team, \mathit{diag})\}$ \Comment{fallback: one whole-team revision}
  \EndIf
  \State $\Team^{*} \gets$ the team in $\mathcal{R}$ with the fewest diagnostics
  \If{$|\textsc{Admit}(\Team^{*})| \le |\mathit{diag}|$} \Comment{never accept a worse team}
    \State $(\Team, \mathit{diag}) \gets (\Team^{*}, \textsc{Admit}(\Team^{*}))$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $(\Team, \mathit{diag})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{How to read Algorithm~\ref{alg:build}.} Phase~1 (lines~1--7) samples up
to $k_{\mathit{prop}}$ proposals. Every proposal is decoded under the JSON Schema
of the typed-team format, so it always has the \emph{shape} of a typed team;
whether it is well typed, complete and anchored remains the checker's decision.
Because a check takes milliseconds (Section~\ref{sec:res-prev}), the checker
can rank the candidates by their number of diagnostics (line~7). Phase~2
(lines~8--17) revises the best candidate for at most $k_{\mathit{adm}}$ rounds.
Line~10 maps each diagnostic to the \emph{fragments} of the team it concerns: a
rule together with the classes it reads and creates and the sources of its
hand-off, a view, the write rights $\omega$, the tools $\kappa$, the obligations
$G$ and the deliverable $\delta$, or $\varphi$; for an unanchored goal, the
fragments include the rules and views that should carry it. Line~11 lists, for
each diagnosed rule, short navigation paths that type-check from its source
variables, which addresses the most frequent error, an undeclared feature.
Line~12 asks the LLM $k_{\mathit{rev}}$ times for replacements of these
fragments only, and the operator $\lhd$ puts them back into $\Team$
deterministically (it is the special case of $\Team\oplus\Delta$ in which the
delta replaces named fragments), so a part that was not diagnosed is never regenerated. Only
if no localised revision reduces the number of violations is one whole-team
revision tried (lines~13--14). A revision is accepted only if it is not worse
than the current team (lines~16--17). We designed the localised revision
because, in pilots, small builders often re-introduced an earlier violation
when they rewrote the whole team. A small ablation does not confirm that it
helps: for Qwen2.5-Coder 7B on 40 ClassEval tasks (one seed), a builder that
makes one proposal and revises the whole team admitted slightly more sessions
than Algorithm~\ref{alg:build} (28 against 21; 14 against 7 discordant tasks,
exact McNemar $p=.19$).

\subsection{Attribution and checked repair}\label{sec:app-attr}

Transcript-based methods identify the responsible agent of a failure in about
half of the cases and the decisive step in about one in seven
(Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). \auto{} separates two questions. \emph{Where}
does a failure show? This is the \emph{symptom} binding, and the trace answers
it by lookup. \emph{Why} did it fail? This is the \emph{cause}, which may lie
in another binding, and a bounded replay answers it.

\paragraph{Locate} If $\varphi$ fails, the engine knows which clauses fail
and, through the trace models, where. A failed $\mathit{valid}$ clause or an
escalation names one pair $(t,b)$: a target object and a stochastic binding,
and with them its rule, hand-off and owner, and the stamped footprint its LLM
saw. A failed $\mathit{cover}(G)$ clause names, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}, a binding on the anchoring chain. Locating the
symptom is therefore a lookup, not an inference over a transcript. Attribution
needs the input of each step~\cite{chen2026traceelephant}; in \tool{}, that
input is a declared, bounded footprint.

\paragraph{Classify} The symptom binding is not always the cause. We
distinguish five fault classes, each with its remedy:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \textsc{Validator}: the check itself is unreliable. Remedy: replace the
validator (builder).
\item \textsc{Sampling}: the composition is adequate, but the owner's LLM was
unlucky or too weak. Remedy: retry, or use a stronger model.
\item \textsc{Footprint}$(j)$: the LLM did not see information it needed, an
instance of \D{2}. Remedy: widen the footprint by the paths, of at most $j$
references, that make replays pass.
\item \textsc{Upstream}$(b')$: an input of the binding, produced by another
stochastic binding $b'$, is wrong. Remedy: re-sample $b'$, after attributing
it in turn.
\item \textsc{Specification}: no value that the footprint supports satisfies
the prompt and the validator. Remedy: a builder delta.
\end{itemize}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} tests these classes in a fixed order, from the cheapest
test to the most expensive one, and \textsc{Specification} is the default when
no test succeeds.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Attribute}: classifying the fault behind a failed value $(t,b)$}\label{alg:attr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require failed value $v$ of binding $b$ of rule $r$ in match $m$; recorded footprint $\mathit{Fp} = \fp_b(m)$; replay cap $n_{\mathit{rep}}$; hop limit $h$;
\Statex \hspace{\algorithmicindent} stability re-runs $n_{\mathit{val}}$; acceptance test $\mathit{ok}(\cdot)$, by default $\mathit{ok}(w) = \chk_b(w, \text{values at } \mathit{vr}_b(m), M_t)$
\Ensure a fault class, with the information its repair needs
\If{re-running $\mathit{ok}(v)$ $n_{\mathit{val}}$ times does not reproduce the recorded verdict}
  \State \Return \textsc{Validator} \Comment{the check is unstable; no LLM call}
\EndIf
\If{$\textsc{Replays}(b, \mathit{Fp})$} \Return \textsc{Sampling} \EndIf \Comment{the same input can succeed}
\For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $h$}
  \State $\mathit{Fp}_j \gets \mathit{Fp} \cup \textsc{PathsWithin}(r, j)$ \Comment{paths of at most $j$ references}
  \If{$\textsc{Replays}(b, \mathit{Fp}_j)$} \Return \textsc{Footprint}$(j)$ with the minimal added paths \EndIf
\EndFor
\For{each value $u$ in $\mathit{Fp}$ or $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$ that a stochastic binding $b'$ wrote}
  \State $u' \gets$ the first re-sample of $b'$ that $b'$'s own validator accepts, of at most $n_{\mathit{rep}}$
  \If{$u'$ exists \textbf{and} $\textsc{Replays}(b, \mathit{Fp})$ with $u'$ in place of $u$ in the models} \Comment{in $\mathit{Fp}$ and $\mathit{vr}_b(m)$}
    \State $\mathit{inner} \gets \textsc{Attribute}(b' \text{ with failed value } u,\ \mathit{ok}')$ where $\mathit{ok}'(w)$ holds iff
    \Statex \hspace{2\algorithmicindent} $b'$'s validator accepts $w$ \textbf{and} $b$ passes with $w$ in place of $u$
    \State \Return \textsc{Upstream}$(b')$ with $\mathit{inner}$ \Comment{the cause lies one hand-off earlier}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return \textsc{Specification} \Comment{no supported value satisfies the check}
\Statex
\Function{Replays}{$b, \Psi$} \Comment{adaptive: stop at the first pass}
  \For{$i = 1$ \textbf{to} $n_{\mathit{rep}}$}
    \If{$\mathit{ok}(\textsc{LLM}_{\mathit{owner}(b)}(\pi_b, \text{values at } \Psi))$} \Return \textit{true} \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return \textit{false}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{How to read Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr}.} Lines~1--2 test the validator
without calling an LLM: the failed value is checked again $n_{\mathit{val}}$ times ($n_{\mathit{val}}=3$ in our evaluation), and if
the verdict changes, the check itself is unreliable. Line~3 replays the binding
on its recorded footprint $\mathit{Fp}$: if some replay passes, the composition was
adequate and the owner's LLM was unlucky or weak (\textsc{Sampling}). Lines~4--6
widen the footprint by every path from the rule $r$'s source variables that
follows at most $j$ references and ends in an attribute; if a replay then
passes, the LLM lacked information (\textsc{Footprint}), and the paths that are
needed are kept for the repair (a greedy minimisation drops each added path
whose removal still lets a replay pass). Lines~7--11 test the inputs that other
LLMs produced: each upstream value $u$ is replaced by the first fresh value
$u'$ that passes its own validator, and if $b$ then passes, the upstream value
was the cause. The replacement is made in the models, so both the replays'
footprint and the validator reads of $b$ see $u'$. Only one accepted $u'$ is
tested per upstream value; if this $u'$ is as wrong as $u$ (because $b'$'s
validator is weak), the upstream test fails and the fault falls through to
\textsc{Specification}. The recursive call of line~10 classifies why $u$ was wrong; there, a
candidate $w$ for $u$ counts as passing only if it passes $b'$'s own validator
\emph{and} the downstream binding $b$ then passes, first with its old failed
value and otherwise with one fresh sample. This criterion ties the upstream
diagnosis to the failure that was observed. Line~12 is the default: no value
that the footprint supports satisfies the prompt and the validator, so the
specification itself must change. \textsc{Replays} (lines~13--16) stops at the
first passing sample; we call this the \emph{adaptive} replay budget.

Two cases fall outside the five classes. A failed clause that names no value,
such as a failed library clause, is reported as \textsc{Unlocated}; a coverage
failure on an object for which no binding exists is reported as
\textsc{Coverage}. Both are passed to the builder, like a specification fault.
They did not occur among the injected faults of Section~\ref{sec:res-trace}.

\begin{remark}[Cost of attribution]\label{rem:attrcost}
For one binding $b$, Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} makes at most $n_{\mathit{rep}}$
replays for the sampling test, $h\cdot n_{\mathit{rep}}$ for the footprint
tests, and, for each of the $|\mathit{up}(b)|$ upstream values, at most
$n_{\mathit{rep}}$ re-samples and $n_{\mathit{rep}}$ replays, where $\mathit{up}(b)$ is the set of
upstream values in $b$'s footprint and validator reads: in total at most
$(h+1+2|\mathit{up}(b)|)\,n_{\mathit{rep}}$ LLM calls at the top level, plus
the replays that minimise a footprint repair. Recursion visits at most as many
bindings as the longest path in the hand-off graph. Within a recursive call,
each evaluation of $\mathit{ok}'(w)$ may draw one further sample of the
downstream binding $b$, when $b$'s old value fails with $w$; a recursive call
on $b'$ therefore costs at most twice the bound for $b'$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Checked repair} The planner of Algorithm~\ref{alg:auto} (line~10)
chooses the cheapest repair for each fault report. Sampling and upstream faults
are repaired mechanically, by a retry of $b$ or a re-sample of $b'$; these
repairs change values but not the team, so they need no check. A footprint fault
becomes an automatic delta that widens the footprint by the paths that
attribution found.
Validator, specification, coverage and unlocated faults require a delta proposed
by the builder's LLM, which rewrites only the fragments that the fault reports
name, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:build}. $\Team\oplus\Delta$ denotes the typed team
with $\Delta$'s edits applied. The checker evaluates it with
Algorithm~\ref{alg:admit}, and \textsc{Apply} installs it in the running team
only if it is admitted. As with consistency-preserving
operators~\cite{horcas2023consistency,alomar2021preserving}, deltas differ in
what they preserve, and \textsc{Apply} uses one of three modes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.3em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{in place}, for a delta that changes only rules, prompts, footprints
or validators: the affected values lose their stamps and become stale, and all
other accepted values are kept;
\item \emph{by extension}, for a delta that only adds views, agents or
hand-offs: the compiler generates the new rule modules, so that new agents
receive work for everything already built;
\item \emph{rebuild}, for any other delta, as a free-form builder must always do.
\end{itemize}
Changes flow only from the team model to the runtime, never back: the runtime
is regenerated from the team model.

\paragraph{The running example, end to end} We follow \code{TaskBoard} from
the first proposal to an accepted deliverable.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*., leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Admission.} The first proposal (Listing~\ref{lst:proposal}) is
rejected with five diagnostics (Listing~\ref{lst:diag}). The reviser rewrites
only the named fragments: the hand-offs and views for \W{1} and \W{4}
(including, for the unanchored goal, the rules that read the goal view,
among them \code{Method2Design}, which no diagnostic names directly), the write rights
for \W{2}, $\varphi$ for \W{5}, and the agents' tools for \W{6}. Its revision
replaces \code{Impl2Run} by \code{Method2Test} (Listing~\ref{lst:rules}), gives
the Tester \code{exec}, removes the Developer's write right on Test, adds
\code{m.docstring} to the footprint of \code{Method2Design}, replaces
\code{d.returnType} by \code{d.method.signature}, lets \code{Design2Impl} read
the Test view (joined on the method) with the tests in its footprint and a
\code{passes\_tests} validator, and drops the Tester's claim from $\varphi$.
The revised team is admitted.
\item \emph{Run.} The implementation of \code{mark\_done} escalates after
three samples fail \code{passes\_tests}.
\item \emph{Locate.} Lookup names the pair
$(\mathit{impl}_{\code{mark\_done}},\code{body})$, owned by the Developer.
\item \emph{Classify the implementation.} Replays on the same footprint fail,
and widening the footprint does not help. The upstream test, however, is
suspicious: the Tester's first test called \code{mark\_done(1)} on a fresh
board, without the \code{add\_task} that E2.1 relies on, so every correct
implementation failed it. \code{test\_valid} had accepted that test because it
runs, fails on a stub and asserts every example. The first re-sample of the
test from its own footprint that \code{test\_valid} accepts happens to create
a board first; with this test in place, the replays of the implementation
pass. (Had it again omitted the set-up, this test would have failed and, since
only one accepted re-sample is tried, the fault would have been classified as
\textsc{Specification}.) The fault is \textsc{Upstream},
in $(\mathit{test}_{\code{mark\_done}},\code{code})$, owned by the Tester.
\item \emph{Classify the test.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} recurses on the test,
with the criterion that a candidate test must also let the implementation pass.
This time, none of the three replays of the test on its own footprint adds the
missing setup, so the sampling test fails: the setup is not part of what the
Tester sees, and only an occasional sample guesses it. Widening the footprint
by two references adds \code{m.task.examples.*}, which contains E1.1, and every
replay then passes: a \textsc{Footprint}$(2)$ fault of \code{Method2Test}. The
two steps also show why sampling and footprint faults can be hard to separate:
a missing input that an LLM can sometimes guess makes a footprint fault look
like bad luck.
\item \emph{Repair.} The automatic delta widens that footprint, is admitted,
and is applied in place. Only the test and the implementation of
\code{mark\_done} become stale and are re-sampled, after which $\varphi$ holds.
\end{enumerate}
A stronger \code{test\_valid} that also checked the order of examples would
have rejected the first test.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}\label{sec:app-scope}

\auto{} checks that a team fits \emph{together}, not that its views capture the
task or that its validators are strong. Hand-off graphs are acyclic, LLMs fill
in attributes but do not create structure, and teams whose value lies in
open-ended dialogue do not decompose naturally into views and rules.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

We organize related work into seven lines. For each, we state what it
contributes and where \auto{} differs; Table~\ref{tab:rw} summarizes the
comparison on the properties that matter for composition.

\subsection{Hand-designed LLM multi-agent teams for software engineering}\label{sec:rw-teams}

Role-based teams emulate human development processes. MetaGPT gives roles
structured output documents~\cite{hong2024metagpt}; ChatDev chains role pairs
through development phases~\cite{qian2024chatdev}; Self-Collaboration and
MapCoder split analysis, coding, testing and
debugging~\cite{dong2024selfcollab,islam2024mapcoder}; and AutoGen and
AgentVerse let developers wire arbitrary
topologies~\cite{wu2024autogen,chen2024agentverse}. Two surveys list
coordination and verification among the open
problems~\cite{he2025lma,liu2024agents4se}. These teams have a human architect.
Beyond the document types of MetaGPT, nothing checks before execution that
every requirement reaches a test, that each artefact has one writer, or that
the system rather than an agent decides completion. \auto{} provides these
properties for teams that no human reviews.

\subsection{Automatic team builders and learned orchestration}\label{sec:rw-builders}

\emph{Per-task builders} design a team for each task. CaptainAgent~\cite{song2024captain},
AutoAgents~\cite{chen2024autoagents} and MAS-Zero~\cite{ke2025maszero} rely on
reflector, observer or meta-feedback LLMs; EvoAgent evolves
agents~\cite{yuan2025evoagent}; DyLAN selects them~\cite{liu2024dylan}; and
MetaAgent represents the team as a finite state machine that makes control
explicit~\cite{zhang2025metaagent}. \emph{Offline builders} search for agentic
workflows as code or graphs: ADAS~\cite{hu2025adas}, AFlow~\cite{zhang2025aflow},
GPTSwarm~\cite{zhuge2024gptswarm}, MaAS~\cite{zhang2025maas} and the GNN-based
G-Designer~\cite{zhang2025gdesigner}. \emph{Learned orchestrators} are trained
with reinforcement learning to pick the next agent
(Puppeteer~\cite{dang2025puppeteer}), to write natural-language workflows
(Conductor~\cite{nielsen2026conductor}) or to emit whole teams
(MAS-Orchestra~\cite{ke2026masorchestra}); other work evolves agent graphs or
refines teams from textual feedback~\cite{yang2025agentnet,ma2026ann,he2026tpgo}.

All of these approaches optimise \emph{which} team to build against an
end-to-end score or another LLM's judgement. Code-based workflows are parsed
and executed, and MetaAgent checks its state machine for reachability. None,
however, states as a checkable interface what is handed over between each pair
of agents, and none checks data-flow coverage or the ownership of hand-offs
before the team runs. \auto{} is complementary: it proposes no new search
strategy but a target representation that any builder can emit, and a check
that a builder can run inside its loop in milliseconds, with diagnostics that
name a rule, path or write right.

\subsection{Failure analysis and attribution for multi-agent systems}\label{sec:rw-failures}

MAST derives 14 failure modes from more than 1{,}600 annotated
traces~\cite{cemri2025mast}, and AgentAsk locates errors on hand-off
edges~\cite{lin2026agentask}. Further studies analyse why agent frameworks
fail, how team structure must fit the task, and why communication does not
imply
coordination~\cite{lu2025exploring,kim2025scaling,pappu2026experts,zhang2026silobench,zhu2025multiagentbench,lamalfa2025miss}.
Who\&When and
TraceElephant attribute failures to agents and steps from
transcripts~\cite{zhang2025whowhen,chen2026traceelephant}. These works
characterise failures after the fact and from prose. \auto{} prevents a class
of them before execution and, when a typed team fails, locates the symptom by
lookup and classifies it by bounded replay. Our defect analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:defects}) builds on their taxonomies and data, and RQ2 uses
their attribution methods as baselines.

\subsection{Structured communication and typed LLM programming}\label{sec:rw-structured}

Agent communication languages gave messages explicit performatives and shared
ontologies long before LLMs~\cite{finin1994kqml}. For LLM agents, MCP and A2A
standardise the transport between agents and tools~\cite{mcp2024,a2a2025};
Agora lets agents negotiate protocol documents~\cite{marro2024agora}; and
PatchBoard grounds collaboration in schema-validated patches to a shared
state~\cite{zhang2026patchboard}. In PatchBoard, an Architect LLM writes a
task-specific blueprint, consisting of a state schema and per-worker read and
write contracts on paths of that state; a deterministic kernel checks that the
blueprint is well formed and then validates every patch against the schema and
the worker's write contract before it commits it, and it logs every commit.
PatchBoard is thus LLM-built and enforces write permissions, but per patch, at
run time, on one shared state. Constrained decoding guarantees that outputs
conform to a grammar or JSON schema~\cite{willard2023outlines}. Practitioner
frameworks type hand-offs too: LangGraph declares a typed state schema for a
graph of agents~\cite{langgraph2025}, CrewAI validates a task's output against
a Pydantic model~\cite{crewai2025}, and AgentSpec enforces user-written rules on
agent actions at run time~\cite{wang2026agentspec}.

These approaches make an individual \emph{message} or action well formed,
reliably transported or permitted. They do not check properties of the
\emph{assembled team}: that every obligation reaches a check that reads it,
that every artefact has one writer, that completion is decided by the system,
or that work goes to an agent with the needed tools. A single shared schema
also removes heterogeneity by decree, whereas \auto{} keeps one view per agent,
sized to its job, and records correspondences in trace models. PatchBoard's
write contracts come closest to \W{2}, but they are enforced on each patch
during the run, and nothing checks before the run that every path has a
single writer or that every part of the task reaches a check; \W{2} and
\W{4} decide both statically, across exogenous transformations between
distinct views. We use a PatchBoard-style shared schema as a baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:design}).

Closest to the \tool{} runtime are LLM programming systems that interleave
deterministic control with generated parts: LMQL constrains and scripts
generation inside a query language~\cite{beurerkellner2023lmql}, SGLang
executes structured LM programs efficiently~\cite{zheng2024sglang}, and DSPy
composes LM modules whose inputs and outputs are typed by
signatures~\cite{khattab2024dspy}. These systems control \emph{how} one program
calls an LLM. DSPy's signatures and assertions come closest to our hand-offs:
they type the inputs and outputs of one module and can reject an output. What a
signature does not fix is \emph{how many} values exist and \emph{which} goal
objects they belong to. \tool{} fixes both by model-transformation rules: a
matched rule creates one target per goal method, so no LLM can forget to test
\code{mark\_done}, and a trace link records the match behind each value. These
are the properties on which the feature-level coverage check (\W{4}) and
attribution by lookup rely, together with three more that \tool{} adds: each
value has a declared footprint, is accepted only behind a validator and is
stamped for incremental re-sampling, and the engine, not an agent, decides
completion.

\subsection{Static analysis, verification and testing of model transformations}\label{sec:rw-mt}

MDE has a mature body of work on checking individual transformations. Static
analysers for ATL detect typing errors, unresolved bindings, rule conflicts and
missing mandatory features~\cite{cuadrado2017static}; invariant-based and
model-finding approaches verify declarative transformations against
contracts~\cite{cabot2010invariants,rahim2015survey}; mutation analysis
assesses transformation test suites~\cite{mottu2006mutation}; and a survey
covers testing and debugging~\cite{troya2022survey}. Fault localisation ranks
suspicious rules statically or from test
spectra~\cite{burgueno2015static,troya2018sbfl}, and anATLyzer proposes
speculatively checked quick fixes~\cite{cuadrado2018quickfix}. Transformation
intents make the properties a transformation must satisfy
explicit~\cite{lucio2016intents}. \W{1}--\W{3} are deliberately close to
established static checks~\cite{cuadrado2017static}. Our diagnostics play the
role of anATLyzer's quick-fix hints, but the fixes are proposed by an LLM and
re-checked; and unlike spectrum-based localisation~\cite{troya2018sbfl}, which
ranks rules by test outcomes, attribution in \auto{} replays one binding on its
recorded footprint. We adopt mutation analysis~\cite{mottu2006mutation} to
evaluate the checker itself.

Closer to the \emph{team} level, transformation chains have been typed and
checked for the compatibility of their connected
metamodels~\cite{vanhooff2007uniti}, and chains between incompatible metamodels
have been assembled automatically~\cite{basciani2014chaining}; footprinting
estimates which parts of a model an operation reads~\cite{jeanneret2011footprints}.
This work assumes that a person writes the transformations and that their
execution is deterministic. Our footprints are \emph{declared} rather than
estimated. Chain typing asks whether a chain's metamodels are compatible;
anchored coverage (\W{4}) asks a finer question, namely whether specific
\emph{goal features} flow into both the production and the check of a value.
This question matters most when values are produced by a stochastic author and
accepted by validators, because then a value can pass its check without its
producer ever having seen the goal. Engine-decided completion (\W{5}) and
capability fit (\W{6}) concern the team rather than any single transformation.

\subsection{Large language models in model-driven engineering}\label{sec:rw-llm-mde}

LLMs have been evaluated for domain modelling~\cite{chen2023domainmodeling},
model completion~\cite{chaaben2023fewshot} and UML modelling
tasks~\cite{camara2023chatgptuml}, with useful but imperfect accuracy; an
evaluation of LLM-based domain-modelling assistance reaches a similar
conclusion~\cite{benchaaben2026utility}. Prompting itself can be model
driven~\cite{clariso2023mdpe}, LLMs have completed software model evolution
from edit histories~\cite{tinnes2025evolution}, and mapping studies chart LLM
use across MDE activities and identify LLM-written metamodels and
transformations as an open setting~\cite{dirocco2025llmmde,zhang2027llmmde}.
Recent studies ask LLMs to write transformations in model transformation
languages and report that syntactic and semantic errors remain
frequent~\cite{jiang2026llm4mtls,kazai2025mtllm}. This line evaluates
\emph{individual} LLM-generated models or transformations. \auto{} accepts that
a proposal will be imperfect and checks how the generated views and
transformations fit together; RQ3 measures how often an LLM reaches an
admissible team. Where an LLM performs a model transformation, it replaces the
transformation as a whole~\cite{kazai2025mtllm}; we are not aware of MDE work
in which rules delegate individual bindings to an LLM behind validators, as
\tool{} does.

\subsection{Software architecture and agent-oriented software engineering}\label{sec:rw-arch}

Architectural mismatch, design by contract and specified connectors make the
assumptions between components explicit and
checkable~\cite{garlan1995mismatch,garlan2009mismatch,meyer1992dbc,allen1997connection},
and Gaia gave every agent role responsibilities, permissions and
protocols~\cite{wooldridge2000gaia}. \auto{} transfers these remedies to
systems that an LLM composes without review: write rights play the role of
Gaia's permissions, hand-offs that of connectors, and $\varphi$ that of a
system-level postcondition.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Research questions}\label{sec:rqs}

\auto{} changes how a team is built and run: the builder must write a typed
team, a checker decides whether the team may run, and a trace records how every
value was produced. Such machinery is not free. It costs tokens and time, and a
builder that cannot write an admissible team produces no team at all. The
evaluation therefore asks what an engineer \emph{gets} for this price. We
evaluate the two benefits that the design promises, and the mechanism that
delivers both. For each, we report the price next to the benefit.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.1em, style=nextline, itemsep=3pt]
\item[\RQ{1} (Reliability): When an \auto{} team reports that it is done, can
we believe it, and when it fails, does it say so?]
Every condition in our study has its own way of ending a run: an agent stops,
a team sends \code{TERMINATE}, or the engine finds the acceptance predicate $\varphi$
true (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}, last column). A developer who uses a team
without re-checking every output relies on this signal. A failure that the
team reports as a success is the costly case, because nobody looks at it. We
call it a \emph{silent failure}. RQ1 compares, over all 44{,}352 runs, how often
each condition fails silently, how often a failed run is flagged as unfinished,
and how much a ``done'' raises the chance that the result is correct.
\emph{Price measured:} task success, how often a run reports ``done'' at all,
and tokens.

\item[\RQ{2} (Traceability): When an \auto{} team fails, can the failure be
traced to the responsible part of the team, explained and repaired there?]
In a free-form team, a failure leaves a chat transcript, and finding the
responsible agent means reading it. In \auto{}, every value has a trace link to
the rule, the agent and the inputs that produced it
(Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}). RQ2 injects known faults into running teams and
asks whether trace-based attribution finds the responsible binding and names
the kind of fault more accurately than four transcript-based methods from the
literature. It also asks whether a checked repair of the diagnosed part works
as well as rebuilding the whole team.
\emph{Price measured:} LLM calls and time spent on attribution.

\item[\RQ{3} (Prevention): Does the admission check catch composition defects
before the team runs, and what does it cost?]
Both benefits rest on the checker: RQ1's reliable ``done'' requires that the
goal is covered by executed checks (\W{4}, \W{5}), and RQ2's lookup requires
that the trace is well formed. RQ3 tests the checker on 151{,}516 mutants of
builder-generated teams, compares it with LLM critics on independently seeded
defects, and measures its run time.
\emph{Price measured:} sessions in which the builder never produces an
admissible team, and milliseconds of checking.
\end{description}

\noindent Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} then collects the price that matters most
to practitioners, task success, and reports a pre-registered experiment that
tested whether a larger budget closes the success gap. It does not.

\paragraph{What we do not claim} We also planned to measure how often
composition defects are the \emph{decisive} cause of a failure and whether
\auto{} reduces such failures. Answering this requires labelling the
cause of each failure. Our two LLM coders disagreed on the defect class in
65.6\% of 5{,}580 sampled failures (Cohen's $\kappa=0.16$), and we did not have
the resources for human coding at this scale. We therefore make no claim about
the causes of failures, and we drop the per-defect shares.

\paragraph{Models and rules} \auto{} and \tool{} are written in Python. The
compiler turns every view of an admitted team into a metamodel in
PyEcore\footnote{\url{https://github.com/pyecore/pyecore}}, an implementation of
EMF's Ecore for Python, and every hand-off into a rule module in a small textual
rule language with ATL's concrete syntax for matched rules, which the engine
parses and interprets. Guards, structural bindings and footprints use an OCL
fragment of navigation paths, literals, comparisons and conjunction. A footprint
path that ends in an object, rather than an attribute, stands for all
attributes of that object.

\paragraph{Builder} A typed team is exchanged as JSON, and the JSON Schema of
this format plays the role of the typed-team metamodel: every proposal and every
revision is decoded under it (Algorithm~\ref{alg:build}, lines~4 and~12). The
schema marks every property as required, because constrained decoding never
emits an optional property and would otherwise never produce a guard. The
builder's prompt library contains this schema, the validator library, three
worked examples (Section~\ref{sec:design}) and, for revisions, the navigation
paths produced by \textsc{TypedPaths}, which follow at most two references. The
anchor features of an obligation default to all attributes of its goal class.

\paragraph{Runtime} The prompt of a stochastic binding consists of the owner's
role prompt, $\pi_b$, a hint on the expected output format, and the footprint
serialised as \emph{object.feature = value} lines. \textsc{RunTeam} stops after
$p_{\max}=4$ passes even if a model still changes; a binding that is still
missing then counts as escalated. A footprint whose $\ksamp$ samples were all
rejected is remembered, so that the binding is not sampled again until its
footprint changes. The deliverable $\delta=(D,g)$ names a target class and
feature, here \cls{MethodImpl}.body; the runtime assembles the deliverable by
inserting every value of $g$ into the class outline of $M_0$ (for
function-level tasks, into the function signature). Behaviour validators run on
this assembled artefact, so that a method is tested together with its siblings.
Methods without an accepted value yet are replaced by stubs, and examples run
in docstring order on one object, as doctest does, so that E2.1 can rely on the
task that E1.1 added. A consequence is that a validator's verdict also depends on
the sibling values, which are not part of the stamp; the clause
$\mathit{valid}$ therefore re-checks every value at the end of the run
(Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}, \emph{Sibling values}). When a
method escalates, the deliverable still contains its last rejected candidate,
so that every condition is scored on the code it produced; such a run never
says done. Validators that execute code run in a separate process with
resource limits and a time-out. The runtime does not restrict a validator to its
owner's declared tools; tool sets are enforced only statically, by \W{6}.

\paragraph{Validators} \code{test\_valid} decides whether a test ``asserts
every example of $m$'' by checking that the test contains each example's call
and expected value as literals; this is a syntactic approximation, not a proof
that the assertion is correct. Its other two parts, that the test runs and that
it fails on a stub of $m$, are executed.

\paragraph{Attribution and repair} We use a replay cap of $n_{\mathit{rep}}=3$
with the adaptive budget, a hop limit of $h=2$, and three re-runs of the
validator for the stability test. Per repair round, at most two failures are
attributed, escalations first, then $\mathit{valid}$ and then
$\mathit{cover}(G)$ failures. A retry clears the binding's escalation mark;
it does not remove the stamp of a value that was accepted and later failed
$\mathit{valid}$ (Section~\ref{sec:app-runtime}), a gap of the evaluated
implementation that can only cost success, because such a run never says
done.

Replays in attribution are drawn without the rejection feedback of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:handoff}, line~21, so they sample from the footprint
alone. We chose this so that each test of Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} asks one
question about one input: whether the \emph{declared} footprint (or a
widened or repaired one) supports an acceptable value. With feedback, a replay
would also see the validator's message, which may mention data outside the
footprint, and a pass could no longer be credited to the footprint. The
choice has a cost. Under the runtime's sampling, a value that is accepted only
at the second or third try, after feedback, has a lower pass rate in replay,
so attribution is biased away from \textsc{Sampling} and towards the later
classes, \textsc{Specification} in particular. The injected sampling faults of
Section~\ref{sec:res-trace} do not expose this bias, because their failure
probability does not depend on the prompt. On the natural failures of the
main study (3{,}500 and 2{,}196 attributed faults of \auto{} runs),
attribution classified 78.7\% (ClassEval) and 83.4\% (HumanEval+) as
\textsc{Specification}, 6.7\% and 5.9\% as \textsc{Sampling}, 7.7\% and
4.5\% as \textsc{Footprint}, 5.9\% and 5.2\% as \textsc{Upstream}, and at most
0.6\% as \textsc{Validator}; we cannot tell how much of
this skew comes from the missing feedback. Replays that reproduce the
runtime's multi-turn feedback are a direct variant that we did not evaluate.

\emph{frozen} version produced every result of

\subsection{Experimental design}\label{sec:design}

We follow guidelines for experiments in software
engineering~\cite{wohlin2012experimentation} and for empirical studies involving
LLMs~\cite{wagner2025guidelines,sallou2024breaking}: local open-weight models,
fixed versions and decoding parameters, several seeds, all prompts and logs
released, and cost reported alongside quality.

\paragraph{Subjects} \emph{ClassEval}~\cite{du2024classeval} contains 100
manually crafted Python class-level tasks with 412 methods and, on average, 33.1
hidden tests per class; a task succeeds only if \emph{all} hidden tests of the
class pass. Class-level tasks are where a team can plausibly help, because
methods interact and must be designed, implemented and tested consistently.
\emph{HumanEval+}~\cite{liu2023evalplus} contains the 164 HumanEval
problems~\cite{chen2021codex} with tests extended by EvalPlus. It represents
small, self-contained tasks for which a team should add little. Teams see only
the prompt, signatures, docstrings and the examples in the docstrings, which we
call \emph{public examples}; hidden tests are used
only for scoring.

\paragraph{Models} We use seven instruction-tuned models from six families, all
run locally through Ollama with 4-bit weights (tags in the package):
Qwen2.5-Coder 7B and 32B~\cite{hui2024qwen25coder}, the agentic
Devstral 24B~\cite{rastogi2025devstral}, the general-purpose
Gemma~3 27B~\cite{gemmateam2025gemma3}, Granite-Code
20B~\cite{mishra2024granitecode}, Code Llama 13B~\cite{roziere2023codellama} and
the completion-oriented StarCoder2 15B~\cite{lozhkov2024starcoder2}. We chose
them to vary the ability to emit a long, schema-conforming specification. We had
planned to use DeepSeek-Coder-V2 16B~\cite{deepseek2024coderv2}; under Ollama
its generation stalled (requests timed out with a single output token), so we
replaced it with Gemma~3 for this technical reason, before any of its results
were available.
In every configuration, the builder, all agents and the repair builder use the
same model.

\paragraph{Conditions} Table~\ref{tab:conditions} defines eight conditions.
Single is the reference that every team must beat. Single-Gate is
compute-matched to \auto{} in its \emph{budget}: it may spend up to \auto{}'s
median output tokens for the same model and benchmark, and it stops at the first
candidate that passes the public examples. Because it often stops early, it
spends fewer tokens on average than \auto{} (15.9k against 21.5k output tokens
per ClassEval run). It also executes checks before declaring success, which
lets us ask whether \auto{}'s reliability comes from executing checks alone. Free, Critic and Schema
are re-implementations in the style of published multi-agent systems with the
same model, tools and examples, because those systems target other benchmarks
and proprietary models; Critic's LLM reviews the team's JSON-rendered
specification for \D{1}--\D{5}, and Free follows CaptainAgent. The two ablations isolate parts of \auto{}.
Typed-NC runs one schema-constrained proposal of the builder (re-prompted only
if the JSON does not parse) with no checker, revision, attribution or repair;
ill-typed bindings escalate, agent claims in $\varphi$ are ignored, and the
runtime stops after $p_{\max}=3$ passes (4 for \auto{}), because an unchecked
team may contain cycles. It thus keeps the typed \emph{format} and the
engine-decided completion but removes the whole build--check--repair loop, so it measures the joint contribution of
checking, ranking, revision and repair, not of the checker alone.
Section~\ref{sec:res-rel} separates static admission from repair in the
existing logs, by splitting Typed-NC runs by whether the checker would have
admitted their team and \auto{} runs by whether they said done before any
repair. Typed-Ref
replaces the \emph{builder} by a hand-written team; the same ClassEval team is
used for HumanEval+, where each task has a single method.

\paragraph{Procedure} Every condition runs on every task of both benchmarks
with every model and three seeds: $8\times7\times264\times3=44{,}352$ runs, on
NVIDIA A100 GPUs of a national research cluster (Ollama version, model digests
and job scripts are in the package). Temperatures are 0.2 for agents and
bindings and 0.6 for the builder; outputs are capped at 4{,}096 tokens; the
runtime draws up to $\ksamp=3$ samples per binding; the builder samples
$k_{\mathit{prop}}=3$ proposals and $k_{\mathit{rev}}=2$ revisions per round
for at most $k_{\mathit{adm}}=3$ rounds; and \auto{} and Typed-Ref make at most
$k_{\mathit{rep}}=2$ repair rounds, with two-sided \W{4}.
Builders receive three structurally different worked examples, with 2, 3 and 4
agents and none taken from either benchmark. In a pilot, one example's analyst
asked for ``edge cases and error conditions'', and the builders' teams copied
this into tests that demand exceptions the task never specifies. We reworded
that example, in both its typed and its prose rendering, to ask only for
documented behaviour, before the main runs.

\paragraph{Version of \auto{} and a post-hoc revision} All results in
Sections~\ref{sec:res-rel}--\ref{sec:res-prev} use the version of \auto{} that
was frozen before the main runs. After seeing them, we revised \auto{} to raise
its success (three changes, described in Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}). Because
the revision was designed with knowledge of the results, we piloted it only on
a development subset (20\% of each benchmark, drawn with a fixed seed when the
revision began), evaluate it on the remaining 80\% of the tasks in a
pre-registered experiment, and report it separately. These \emph{held-out}
tasks were not used to pilot the revision, but they were part of the main
study whose aggregate results motivated it. The baselines were not changed.

\paragraph{Metrics} \emph{Success}: all hidden tests pass. Success is scored
on the code a run produced, whether or not the run says done: for an \auto{}
run that ends with \textsc{Failed}, this is the deliverable with the last
rejected candidate of each escalated method (Section~\ref{sec:impl}); a refused
session has no code and fails. Success therefore counts $\mathit{TP}$ and
$\mathit{FN}$ runs alike; a user who trusts the completion signal would keep
only the $\mathit{TP}$ runs. For RQ1 we treat
each condition's own completion signal (``says done'') as a classifier of
success. A \emph{run} is one condition on one task with one model and seed; for
\auto{}, it starts with one builder \emph{session}, so sessions and runs
correspond one to one. Every run then falls into one of four cells: it says done and passes
($\mathit{TP}$), says done and fails ($\mathit{FP}$), does not say done but
passes ($\mathit{FN}$), or does not say done and fails ($\mathit{TN}$). With $n$
runs, we report:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Silent-failure rate} $\mathit{FP}/n$: the share of \emph{all} runs
that say done but fail, that is, the rate at which a user who trusts the signal
accepts a wrong result.
\item \emph{Flagged failures} $\mathit{TN}/(\mathit{TN}+\mathit{FP})$: the share
of failed runs that do not say done, that is, failures the condition admits.
\item \emph{Precision of ``done''} $\mathit{TP}/(\mathit{TP}+\mathit{FP})$: the
share of runs that say done that pass.
\item \emph{Sensitivity of ``done''} $\mathit{TP}/(\mathit{TP}+\mathit{FN})$: the
share of successful runs that say done. Its complement is the share of correct
results that a user who trusts the signal would discard.
\item \emph{Matthews correlation coefficient} (MCC) between ``says done'' and
success, which summarizes all four cells in one number that does not reward
saying done rarely or often~\cite{chicco2020mcc}.
\end{itemize}


The first two measures improve when a condition says done less often; precision
can too, if a condition says done only on easy tasks. We therefore read them
together with sensitivity and MCC, and report how often each condition says
done. \emph{Cost}: input and output tokens and wall-clock time per run.
Attribution and detection measures are defined in their sections.

\paragraph{Statistical analysis} Runs are clustered by task, because the same
task appears in every condition, model and seed. All intervals on proportions
are 95\% percentile intervals from a task-clustered bootstrap with 1{,}000
resamples~\cite{efron1993bootstrap}, unless stated otherwise; Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927} are used for small samples. Per model and
benchmark, success is compared by exact McNemar
tests~\cite{mcnemar1947} on task-level majority outcomes over the three seeds,
Holm-corrected within each family of seven comparisons~\cite{holm1979}. Paired
comparisons of attribution and repair use exact McNemar tests. The
pre-registered experiment of Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} uses logistic models
fitted by generalised estimating equations (GEE), clustered by
task~\cite{liang1986gee}. We use $\alpha=0.05$.

\subsection{RQ1: Can the team's ``done'' be trusted?}\label{sec:res-rel}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig-rq1-outcomes.tex}

signal, pooled over seven models and three seeds (ClassEval: 2{,}100 runs per
condition; HumanEval+: 3{,}444). Black: the run says done but fails the hidden
tests (\emph{silent failure}); its whisker is the 95\% task-clustered bootstrap
interval and its value is printed on the right. Dark grey: says done and

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} shows, for every condition, how its runs divide into the
four cells of the metrics; Table~\ref{tab:silent-model} in the appendix gives
the silent-failure rate per model. We report five findings.

\paragraph{(1) \auto{} has the fewest silent failures of all conditions}
5.9\% [4.1, 7.8] of \auto{}'s ClassEval runs and 5.6\% [4.3, 7.0] of its
HumanEval+ runs say done but fail. A single agent fails silently three to four
times as often (17.6\% [12.5, 22.5] and 20.4\% [16.7, 24.2]), and free-form,
critic and shared-schema teams five to ten times as often (27--56\%). The
pooled result holds per model: \auto{} has fewer silent failures than Single
for six of the seven models on both benchmarks; the seventh, StarCoder2, ties
at zero because its single agent never says done. It has fewer than all three
multi-agent baselines in 11 of the 14 model--benchmark pairs; the exceptions are
Devstral's Free and Critic teams on both benchmarks, which rarely send
\code{TERMINATE} at all, and Qwen2.5-Coder 7B's Schema team on HumanEval+.

\paragraph{(2) When \auto{} fails, it says so} Of the failed \auto{} runs,
93.4\% (ClassEval) and 90.9\% (HumanEval+) end without ``done'': the engine
reports an unmet obligation, an open escalation or a refusal instead of a
result. A failed single-agent run is flagged in 77\% and 48\% of cases, and a
failed multi-agent run in 32--48\%.

\paragraph{(3) Part of the advantage is abstention} \auto{} says done in only
10.5\% (ClassEval) and 38.4\% (HumanEval+) of its runs, against 33.6\% and
80.1\% for Single, and a session whose team is never admitted (39\% of
\auto{}'s ClassEval sessions and 37\% of its HumanEval+ sessions) cannot fail
silently by construction. To separate this effect,
we restrict \auto{} to its admitted sessions (1{,}279 ClassEval and 2{,}178
HumanEval+ runs). Their silent-failure rate is 9.7\% [6.9, 12.8] and 8.8\%
[6.9, 11.3]: higher than over all sessions, but still about half the single
agent's rate. Per model, admitted teams fail silently less often than the
single agent in 11 of the 14 model--benchmark pairs; StarCoder2 ties at zero on
both benchmarks, and Qwen2.5-Coder 7B on HumanEval+ is the one exception (14.0\%
against 13.4\%; Table~\ref{tab:silent-model}). The two builders that are admitted in almost
every session show the same pattern with hardly any refusals to explain it: their
admitted teams fail silently in 15.1\% (Qwen2.5-Coder 32B) and 17.4\% (Devstral)
of ClassEval runs, against 24.3\% and 21.7\% for their single agents, and in
6.6\% and 11.9\% of HumanEval+ runs, against 10.8\% and 20.3\%.

\paragraph{(4) As a signal of success, \auto{}'s ``done'' is the strongest on
HumanEval+ and on par on ClassEval} On HumanEval+, 85.5\% [81.6, 88.9] of
\auto{}'s ``done'' runs pass, the highest precision of all conditions (single
agent 74.5\%, multi-agent teams 56--63\%), and its MCC of 0.75 [0.72, 0.79] is
well above that of every baseline (single agent 0.56 [0.50, 0.61]). On
ClassEval, its precision (43.9\% [31.2, 57.4]) and MCC (0.37 [0.26, 0.46]) are
indistinguishable from the single agent's (47.7\% and 0.38 [0.24, 0.51]) and
about twice those of the multi-agent teams (precision 19--22\%, MCC
0.02--0.15). The price of this caution is sensitivity: only 43.7\% of
\auto{}'s successful ClassEval runs say done (single agent: 65.4\%), so a user
who trusts the signal would discard more than half of its correct results. On
HumanEval+, the corresponding shares are 84.3\% and 97.8\%.

\paragraph{(5) Executing tests before stopping is not enough} Single-Gate runs
the public examples before it declares success, with the same budget cap as
\auto{}, yet it fails silently at least as often as Single (21.5\% on both
benchmarks). Passing the public examples is a weaker signal than $\varphi$,
which requires every declared obligation to have been checked and every
accepted value to be re-validated on the final deliverable. The ablation
Typed-NC, which keeps the typed format and $\varphi$ but removes the
build--check--repair loop, fails silently more often than \auto{} when pooled
(10.1\% against 5.9\% on ClassEval; 12.7\% against 5.6\% on HumanEval+) and has
lower precision (25.0\% against 43.9\%; 68.7\% against 85.5\%). Per model,
however, the picture is mixed (Table~\ref{tab:silent-model}). Typed-NC has
\emph{fewer} silent failures than \auto{} in 6 of the 14 model--benchmark pairs,
including both strong builders on both benchmarks (for example 6.3\% against
14.7\% for Qwen2.5-Coder 32B on ClassEval), and as many or more in the others.
The pooled difference comes mainly from Granite-Code, Code Llama and StarCoder2,
whose unchecked teams fail silently in 7--36\% of runs while their \auto{}
sessions are mostly refused or never reach $\varphi$.

Because the ablation removes the whole loop, we separated its parts in the
existing logs, without new LLM runs. \emph{Static admission:} we ran the
checker on every Typed-NC team as the builder wrote it. Teams that the
checker would have admitted (801 ClassEval and 1{,}339 HumanEval+ runs) fail
silently in 6.4\% and 6.2\% of runs, with a precision of ``done'' of 52.8\%
and 89.7\%; teams it would have rejected (1{,}299 and 2{,}105 runs) fail
silently in 12.5\% and 16.8\%, with a precision of 8.0\% and 40.2\%, and
succeed far less often (3.9\% against 17.5\%; 17.7\% against 64.4\%). This
split is observational, because weak builders write most of the rejected
teams; within the two strong builders, rejected teams do not fail silently
more often (for example Qwen2.5-Coder 32B on ClassEval: 0.0\% of 39 rejected
against 7.3\% of 261 admissible runs), so the checker's verdict mainly
separates the weak builders' teams. \emph{Runtime repair:} a run that says
done before any repair round ($j=0$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:auto}) ends there,
so the silent failures that repair adds can be counted. On ClassEval, repair
rounds turned 27 runs into correct ``done'' runs and 52 into silent failures;
on HumanEval+, 116 and 34. Before repair, admitted \auto{} teams fail
silently in 5.6\% (ClassEval) and 7.3\% (HumanEval+) of runs, against 9.7\%
and 8.8\% after it. This explains the reversal for the strong builders: for
Qwen2.5-Coder 32B on ClassEval, \auto{} fails silently in 6.7\% of runs before
repair, about as often as Typed-NC (6.3\%), and repair raises this to 14.7\%;
for Devstral, from 12.3\% to 17.0\% (Typed-NC: 11.0\%). Of the 52 ClassEval
runs, 25 involved the re-sampling of an upstream value and 29 a builder delta:
a re-sampled test that the weak \code{test\_valid} accepts, or a delta that
rewrites a test or validator, can let an implementation pass its checks while
it still fails the hidden tests. The admission check thus helps where builders
are weak, and repair on ClassEval trades silent failures for a few successes.
We therefore read the ablation as evidence that the typed format and
engine-decided completion carry much of the reliability by themselves, that
static admission mainly protects against weak builders, and that runtime
repair, as implemented, costs reliability on class-level tasks. For the same reason, the hand-written
Typed-Ref team, which is checked but used for every task, can fail silently more
often (12.9\% on ClassEval) than the unchecked Typed-NC teams, which rarely reach
$\varphi$ at all (13.5\% say done, against 21.8\% for Typed-Ref).

\paragraph{The price} \auto{}'s reliability comes with a more conservative
``done'' and with lower success. It succeeds in 10.6\% (ClassEval) and 38.9\%
(HumanEval+) of runs, against 24.5\% and 61.0\% for a single agent
(Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} explains why). It spends 7 times (ClassEval) and 16
times (HumanEval+) the output tokens of a single agent (21.5k against 2.9k and
12.7k against 0.8k per run on average), and 14 times its input tokens on
ClassEval (65.8k against 4.6k), because every binding is prompted separately
with its footprint.

\begin{answerbox}\textbf{Answer to RQ1.} Largely yes. Of the eight conditions,
\auto{} has the fewest silent failures on both benchmarks (5.9\% and 5.6\% of
runs, a third to a quarter of a single agent's rate) and flags more than 90\%
of its failures. Part of this comes from abstention, but admitted teams still
fail silently about half as often as a single agent (9.7\% and 8.8\%). The
typed format alone, without the build--check--repair loop, already removes much
of the gap; static admission helps mainly with weak builders, and on
ClassEval runtime repair adds more silent failures than successes. Its
``done'' is the most reliable signal of success on HumanEval+ and as reliable as
a single agent's on ClassEval. \emph{Price:} \auto{} says done less often,
discards more than half of its correct ClassEval results, succeeds less often,
and uses 7--16 times the output tokens of a single agent.
\end{answerbox}

\subsection{RQ2: Can failures be traced, explained and repaired?}\label{sec:res-trace}

\paragraph{Setup} To know the true cause of a failure, we injected it. The
study has three parts.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Fault injection.} We took runs that had succeeded and injected one
fault of one of five classes: \textsc{Upstream}, a wrong upstream value accepted
by a weak validator; \textsc{Footprint}, the deliverable's footprint narrowed to
the method signature; \textsc{Specification}, an unsatisfiable validator;
\textsc{Sampling}, the owner's LLM degraded for one method (each answer is
replaced by a broken one with probability 0.6, also during replays, giving pass
rates of about 0.3--0.6); and \textsc{Validator}, a flaky validator. Each fault
is injected into a snapshot of the clean run, and only what the injection makes
stale is re-sampled, so the faulted run differs from the clean run only by the
injection. A fault counts if $\varphi$ then fails at a value that the clean run
had accepted and still held valid at its end (for \textsc{Upstream}, at the
value downstream of the corrupted one). We injected faults into ClassEval runs
of the hand-written Typed-Ref team with Qwen2.5-Coder 7B and 32B and Devstral,
and into runs of builder-generated \auto{} teams with Qwen2.5-Coder 32B. Of
1{,}108 injections, 993 produced a failure (752 in reference teams, 241 in
builder teams; 47--153 per class).
\item \emph{Transcript-based baselines.} Four methods from Who\&When and
TraceElephant~\cite{zhang2025whowhen,chen2026traceelephant} (all at once, step
by step, binary search and agentic replay) received the same 752 faulted
reference-team runs as transcripts with full inputs: one step per LLM call, with
its agent, input, output and the automatic check of the output. All four used
Qwen2.5-Coder 32B as judge and the five classes, with one-line definitions, as a
closed label set.
\item \emph{Repair.} We took 562 failed \auto{} runs and compared one checked
repair of the diagnosed part (Section~\ref{sec:app-attr}) with rebuilding the
whole team from scratch given the same fault report.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(1) The trace locates the responsible part} Table~\ref{tab:attribution}
reports the results; the first three transcript methods come from
Who\&When~\cite{zhang2025whowhen} and agentic replay from
TraceElephant~\cite{chen2026traceelephant}. With 752 failures, every
percentage in the table has a Wilson 95\% interval of at most $\pm3.6$ points
($\pm5.3$ for the 241 builder-team failures), so the differences between trace
and transcript methods are far larger than the noise. The binding where
$\varphi$ fails (the symptom) is found in every case, by construction
(Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}). The trace finds the binding where the fault was
\emph{injected} in 98.3\% of reference-team failures and 96.3\% of builder-team
failures, including 92.5\% and 83.3\% of upstream faults, whose symptom appears
one hand-off later. The best transcript method locates the responsible step in
61.8\% of cases. Results are stable across the three models of the reference
team (responsible binding 96--100\%). The responsible \emph{agent} is named in
78\% of cases, against at most 55\% by transcript methods. The gap between the
binding (98\%) and the agent (78\%) has one cause: every binding has a single
owner (\W{2}), but a validator fault is charged to the check, not to an
agent: its ground truth is ``no agent'', and attribution names no agent when
it blames the validator. Of the 153 reference-team failures with the correct
binding but the wrong agent, 152 are sampling faults taken for validator
faults (65) or the reverse (87). The transcript methods were scored the same
way: they could answer ``(validator)'' instead of an agent. If, instead, every
fault were charged to the owner of the binding whose value failed, the agent
named by the trace would be correct whenever the binding is (98.3\% and
96.3\%).

\paragraph{(2) The trace explains what kind of fault occurred} With adaptive
replay, \auto{} names the fault class correctly in 75.3\% of reference-team
failures and 73.7\% of builder-team failures, more than twice the best
transcript method (32.8\%). Chance is 20\%; the step-by-step method (13.0\%)
stays below it because in 359 of the 752 runs (48\%) it judges no step
decisive and therefore returns no class. Specification, upstream and footprint faults are classified
correctly in 83--100\% of cases. The hard case is telling \textsc{Sampling}
faults (an agent that fails by chance) apart from \textsc{Validator} faults (a
check that fails by chance): both can make a replay pass, so in reference teams
43\% of sampling faults are taken for validator faults and 57\% of validator
faults for sampling faults. The replay budget matters for exactly this case.
With a single replay, sampling faults are recognised in 7\% of cases, because
the injected agents fail a single replay 40--70\% of the time: in 74 of the 152
cases, the one replay on the original footprint fails and the one replay on the
widened footprint passes, so the fault is taken for a footprint fault. With up
to three replays, sampling faults are recognised in 57\% of cases. Macro accuracy rises from 65.7\% to 75.3\% (77 against 4
discordant failures, exact McNemar $p<.001$).

\paragraph{(3) Repairing the diagnosed part is as successful as rebuilding,
with less work} One checked repair made the task pass its hidden tests in
21.5\% [18.3, 25.1] of the 562 failed runs, against 18.9\% [15.8, 22.3] for
rebuilding the team from scratch (difference 2.7 points [$-0.4$, 5.7]; 49
against 34 discordant runs, exact McNemar $p=.12$). The interval excludes a
disadvantage of more than half a point, so repair is not worse than rebuilding
for any practically relevant margin. The repair needed a median of 2{,}309
tokens instead of 4{,}167 (45\% fewer), and it preserved 79\% of the values that
the team had already accepted. Of the repairs, 72\% were applied in place, that
is, only the diagnosed rule or binding changed. Repair was never worse than
rebuilding for any model (Qwen2.5-Coder 7B: 16.9\% against 10.6\%; Devstral:
20.0\% against 18.4\%; Qwen2.5-Coder 32B: 27.9\% for both).

\paragraph{A caveat on the comparison} The five injected classes are the five
classes that Algorithm~\ref{alg:attr} distinguishes, and three of the
injections (flaky validator, unsatisfiable validator, narrowed footprint)
correspond directly to one of its tests. The comparison therefore shows that the
trace and bounded replay recover a known taxonomy of faults far better than a
transcript does; it does not show how well this taxonomy covers natural
failures. The comparison also mixes two kinds of evidence. \emph{Locating}
the symptom is passive: the trace answers it by lookup, without LLM calls,
from what the run recorded. \emph{Classifying} the fault is active: it
re-runs the validator, re-samples bindings and widens footprints along the
metamodel, that is, it executes counterfactuals that a single transcript
cannot show. Of the transcript methods, only agentic replay could act: it
could re-run any step on its recorded input with the subject model, but it
could not re-run validators, query the views or change a footprint. The gap
in fault-class accuracy therefore measures the value of active, model-aware
probing as much as that of the trace. Locating the symptom is due to the
trace alone; locating the responsible binding of an upstream fault, one
hand-off earlier, also uses replays. On the natural failures of the
main study, attribution named \textsc{Specification} for about four in five
faults (Section~\ref{sec:impl}); we have no ground truth for them. The
transcript methods were also adapted to our runs and to a 32B judge rather
than used on their native conversations with the proprietary judges of their
original papers.

\paragraph{The price} Traceability is not free. Adaptive attribution uses 7.7
LLM calls per failure, against one call for the cheapest transcript method; for
Qwen2.5-Coder 32B, attribution takes a median of 793\,s in the runs that invoke
it, which is more than half of the median run (1{,}444\,s), because replays
re-run stochastic bindings. Repair also helps only a minority of failed runs:
four in five failures remain failures after one repair round.

\begin{answerbox}\textbf{Answer to RQ2.} Yes, for the fault classes we
injected. The trace locates the responsible binding of 96--98\% of injected
faults, against at most 62\% (step) for transcript methods, and names the fault
class correctly in 74--75\% of cases, against at most 33\%. A checked repair of
the diagnosed part is as successful as rebuilding the team, uses 45\% fewer
tokens and keeps 79\% of the accepted work. \emph{Price:} about eight LLM calls
per failure; with Qwen2.5-Coder 32B, attribution takes more than half of the
median run time, and four in five failures remain failures after one repair.
\end{answerbox}

\subsection{RQ3: Are composition defects caught before the team runs?}\label{sec:res-prev}

\paragraph{Setup} We test the checker in three ways.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Independently seeded defects.} To avoid testing the checker only on
defects designed for it, Gemma~3 was asked to seed one defect into each of 300
admitted builder teams (60 per defect class). It saw only the plain-language
definitions of \D{1}--\D{5} (Table~\ref{tab:defects}), never \W{1}--\W{6}. When
its edit left the team unchanged, it was told so and asked again, up to four
times; 23 teams that were still unchanged and 2 for which no team came back
were removed, leaving 275 seeded teams (48--59 per class). To measure false alarms, we built a clean set
\emph{without} the checker: 30 hand-written typed teams for ClassEval classes
and 30 teams of the unchecked condition Typed-NC that reached $\varphi$ and
passed the hidden tests. Three LLM critics (Qwen2.5-Coder 7B and 32B, Devstral)
received the same JSON specifications and defect definitions as the seeder.
\item \emph{Mutation of natural teams.} We wrote nine mutation operators, one or
two per defect class, and applied each at every applicable site of the 3{,}379
distinct teams that builders generated and the checker admitted in the main
runs (3{,}457 admitted sessions, some of which produced identical teams),
yielding 151{,}516 mutants (Table~\ref{tab:natmut}). Each mutant was checked
with three variants of \W{4}: \emph{path-only} (some class-level path leads from
each goal class to a behaviour-checked class), \emph{one-sided} anchoring (goal
data must reach the check of a value), and the \emph{two-sided} anchoring of
Definition~\ref{def:anchor} (goal data must reach both the producer and the
check).
\item \emph{Mutation of hand-written teams.} The same operators applied to the
typed \devteam{} and a hand-written requirements team that derives user stories and API operations from a goal model (85 mutants), under two goal
declarations: $G_1$ declares only checked obligations, and $G_2$ also declares
the deliverables.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(1) On independently seeded defects, the checker is the only judge
that separates defective from clean teams} With two-sided anchoring, the checker
rejects 173 of the 275 seeded teams (62.9\%, Wilson 95\% CI [57.1, 68.4]) and
raises false alarms on 8 of the 60 clean teams (13.3\% [6.9, 24.2]; one-sided
and path-only: 11.7\%). Detection depends on the defect class: 98\% for
unverifiable completion (\D{4}), 71\% for coverage gaps (\D{1}), 66\% for
ownership conflicts (\D{3}), 47\% for capability mismatches (\D{5}) and 27\%
for hand-off mismatches (\D{2}). The three variants of \W{4} perform alike
here, because no seeded defect detaches the goal from the producers. The LLM
critics do not discriminate at all. The two larger critics report a defect in
\emph{every} team, seeded or clean, and the 7B critic reports one in 40.4\% of
seeded and 40.0\% of clean teams. A critic that flags every team, or flags
teams at random, gives an engineer no information about a specific team.

\paragraph{What the checker misses, and its false alarms} Reading the
descriptions of the 102 seeded defects that the checker misses, we grouped them
roughly as follows. About 40\% add an agent or a tool that no hand-off uses,
which the seeder labelled as an ownership conflict (\D{3}) or a capability
mismatch (\D{5}). Such an addition does not break the team, so arguably it is
not a composition defect at all; without these cases, detection would be about
74\%. About 20\% change a prompt's wording or a declared output format but no
footprint, for example asking for a return type ``as a string''; the checker
reasons about what an agent reads and writes, not about what its prompt says.
About 15\% remove a guard, which the checker only warns about
(Section~\ref{sec:app-check}). The rest change behaviour that no static check
can see, for example an agent instructed to introduce off-by-one errors. All
eight false alarms are Typed-NC teams, which were written without the checker
and happened to pass their task. Four reference a feature that their views do
not declare (\W{1}), three let two agents write one view (\W{2}), and three
have a producer that does not read the goal (\W{4}); some violate two
rules. These teams passed their task despite the violations. The checker thus rejects some
teams that would have worked; this is the price of a conservative check.

\paragraph{(2) Mutation analysis: a sanity check and a blind spot of weaker
coverage} High detection of mutants is expected by construction, because each
operator breaks a rule that the checker states. The analysis is
nonetheless informative in two ways. First, it confirms that the rules
behave as specified on teams written by LLMs, not only on our own: with
two-sided anchoring, the checker rejects 151{,}496 of the 151{,}516 mutants
(99.99\%); the 20 survivors are 12 tool removals, 7 rule deletions and one
detached team. Second, it exposes a blind spot of weaker formulations of
coverage (Table~\ref{tab:natmut}, second row). The \emph{detach} operator keeps
every rule but removes all goal data from the producers' footprints, so agents
write code without ever seeing the task's examples. Path-only coverage admits
all 3{,}377 detached teams, and one-sided anchoring admits 98.6\% of them,
because the validators still read the examples. Only two-sided anchoring rejects
them. The hand-written teams add a second lesson: under $G_1$, deleting the rule
that creates the requirements team's API operations escapes all variants,
because every declared obligation still reaches a check; declaring the
deliverables ($G_2$) catches it, and two-sided anchoring then rejects all 85
mutants. Coverage is relative to what is declared.

\paragraph{(3) Checking costs milliseconds} Figure~\ref{fig:scale}a shows that
the cost of a check grows polynomially with team size, as
Proposition~\ref{prop:cost} predicts: chain teams of 1{,}000 and 3{,}000 views

its LLM calls, not of its checks.

rejects defective teams also rejects the only teams that a weak builder can

inconsistencies within a single proposal, not poor design decisions. Diagnostic
rounds help little: they add 3--12 points of admission. A refused session is an
honest outcome: the team does not run and does not claim success. It is also
lost work, and one of the two reasons for \auto{}'s lower success
(Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}).

\begin{answerbox}\textbf{Answer to RQ3.} Yes, for the defects it is designed

alarms and is the only judge whose verdict separates defective from clean
teams; the LLM critics flag clean and defective teams alike. On 151{,}516
mutants of LLM-written teams it behaves as specified (99.99\% rejected), and
only two-sided anchoring catches teams whose producers never see the goal. A
check takes milliseconds. \emph{Price:} for four of seven models, 45--81\% of
builder sessions never produce an admissible team.
\end{answerbox}

is the \emph{decomposition} itself: admitted teams succeed less often than a
single agent on ClassEval even for strong builders (Qwen2.5-Coder 32B: 26.0\%
against 36.0\%; Devstral: 23.9\% against 33.0\%), and so does the hand-written
Typed-Ref team, which involves no builder (15.1\% against 24.5\% on average). A
typed team implements each method separately from its footprint, and whatever
the footprint omits, for example how sibling methods share state, a single agent
that sees the whole class can still use. On HumanEval+, where a task is a single
function, admitted teams of strong builders are on par with a single agent
(Qwen2.5-Coder 32B: 87.2\% against 88.0\%; Devstral: 79.5\% against 77.2\%),
and for these two models \auto{} as a whole is on par with the multi-agent
baselines (Qwen2.5-Coder 32B: 83.3\% against 79.7--83.7\%; Devstral: 77.0\%
against 75.0--77.2\%; no significant difference). A
compute-matched single agent with an execution gate (Single-Gate) has the
highest mean success on both benchmarks, in line with reports that the gains of
multi-agent systems over strong single agents are
small~\cite{cemri2025mast,kim2025scaling}.

\paragraph{A post-hoc revision} After seeing these results, we revised \auto{}
in three ways that target the lost work rather than the checker:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=1pt]
\item[(R1)] after admission, the runtime extends the footprint of the
deliverable binding by the goal method's docstring and examples, if the builder
left them out, and adds an \code{examples\_run} validator; this edit only adds
reads and a check, but it is applied to the admitted team without a second
check. It adds the validator only if every writer of the deliverable's view
has the \code{exec} tool, so it cannot introduce a \W{6} violation; the added
paths are features of the goal method that the rule already binds, so they
type-check (\W{1});
\item[(R2)] when a method escalates, the deliverable contains the rejected
candidate that passes the most documented examples, instead of the last
rejected candidate;
\item[(R3)] when the builder's team is not admitted, \auto{} runs a hand-written,
admitted library team (the Typed-Ref team) instead of refusing.
\end{itemize}
The checker, the anchoring criterion and the baselines are unchanged. On the
held-out tasks, the revision raised \auto{}'s success with Qwen2.5-Coder 7B from
9.6\% to 21.2\% (ClassEval) and from 28.8\% to 71.8\% (HumanEval+), and with
Qwen2.5-Coder 32B from 23.8\% to 30.0\% and from 81.9\% to 86.8\%. The frozen
values are the main-study runs on the held-out tasks ($\ksamp=3$); the revised
values are those of Experiment~1 at $\ksamp=1$ (Table~\ref{tab:exp1}), so the
gain is not due to more samples. The comparison is exploratory.

The three changes are bundled, but the logs record which sessions ran the
library team (R3), so its share can be separated. With Qwen2.5-Coder 7B, R3
ran in 98 of 240 ClassEval and 248 of 393 HumanEval+ sessions, and these
sessions contributed 8.8 of the 21.2 points of success on ClassEval and 44.3
of the 71.8 points on HumanEval+. Most of the gain for the weak builder is
thus the hand-written team; the builder's own teams, now with R1 and R2,
contributed 12.5 and 27.5 points, against 9.6 and 28.8 points for the whole
frozen version. With Qwen2.5-Coder 32B, R3 ran in 5 of 240 and 17 of 393
sessions (0.8 and 4.1 points), and the builder's own teams contributed 29.2
points on ClassEval against 23.8, so for the strong builder the gain on
ClassEval comes from R1 and R2. R1 and R2 cannot be separated from each other
in these runs.

The revision also shows the trade-off between success and reliability (same
runs). With Qwen2.5-Coder 32B, it kept the reliability advantage: silent
failures of 7.9\% (ClassEval) and 5.9\% (HumanEval+) against 28.8\% and 10.4\%
for Single-Gate. With Qwen2.5-Coder 7B, silent failures were 6.7\% on
ClassEval (Single-Gate: 23.3\%) but 16.3\% on HumanEval+, up from 5.9\% in the
frozen version and above Single-Gate's 13.5\%. Most of these came from the
fallback: on HumanEval+, the library team failed silently in 20.2\% of the
sessions in which it ran, the builder's own teams in 9.7\%, and the fallback
sessions account for 12.7 of the 16.3 points (on ClassEval, 5.0 of 6.7).
Recovering lost work with a fallback team can thus reintroduce the silent
failures that the checker prevents, when the builder is weak.

\paragraph{Experiment 1: does a larger budget close the gap?} A typed team
validates and repairs each method separately, whereas a single agent must get a
whole class right in one sample. We therefore expected \auto{}'s success to
rise with its budget faster than Single-Gate's, especially for large classes.
We pre-registered this experiment: hypotheses, design and analysis were written
down on 22 October 2026, before any of its runs, and the protocol is in the
package [registry link to be added].
% TODO(author): deposit hpc/docs/preregistration-exp1-budget-scaling.md on OSF (or similar) and cite the timestamped link.
The hypotheses were:
\emph{H1}: on ClassEval, the success of \auto{} rises more steeply with $\log_2 k$
than that of token-matched Single-Gate;
\emph{H2}: the difference is larger for classes with at least six methods than
for classes with at most three;
\emph{H3} (negative control): on HumanEval+ there is no such difference.
We varied the samples per binding $k\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, gave Single-Gate
\auto{}'s median output tokens at each level as its budget cap, used the revised
\auto{} (with R1--R3, so a refused session runs the library team), and ran on
the held-out tasks with three seeds. Of the 80 held-out
ClassEval classes, 30 have at most three methods and 13 at least six. For each
benchmark and model, we fitted a logistic GEE with an exchangeable working
correlation, clustered by task, with the terms condition, $\log_2 k$ and their
interaction (for H2, also class size and all its interactions); the interaction
coefficient is on the log-odds scale per doubling of $k$.

The hypotheses are not supported (Table~\ref{tab:exp1}; for reference, a
single agent reaches 35.8\% and 37.1\% on ClassEval and 84.0\% and 88.0\% on
HumanEval+ with the 7B and 32B model on the same tasks, and a free-form team
21.7\%, 27.9\%, 69.0\% and 78.9\%). For Qwen2.5-Coder
7B, \auto{}'s success on ClassEval does not rise with the budget; it falls
slightly from 21.2\% to 17.5\% while its output tokens grow from 21.9k to 30.9k.
The condition~$\times$~$\log_2 k$ interaction is $-0.06$ (95\% CI $[-0.17,
0.04]$, odds ratio 0.94 per doubling, $p=.24$; H1 not supported); the interval
rules out a gain of more than 0.04 on the log-odds scale per doubling, and the three-way interaction with class
size is $0.09$ ($p=.40$; H2 not supported). Both conditions plateau: the change
from $k=4$ to $k=8$ is $-1.3$ points $[-5.4, 2.5]$ for \auto{} and $-2.5$
points $[-5.8, 0.4]$ for Single-Gate. Qwen2.5-Coder 32B shows the same flat
pattern on ClassEval: \auto{} stays between 29.2\% and 31.2\% while its output
tokens grow from 17.5k to 24.7k, and Single-Gate stays between 40.4\% and
41.2\%. The interaction is $0.01$ ($[-0.08, 0.10]$, $p=.77$; H1 not supported),
and the three-way interaction with class size is $0.17$ ($p=.24$; H2 not
supported). On HumanEval+, as expected under H3, no slope difference appears
(Qwen2.5-Coder 7B: $0.04$, $p=.37$; Qwen2.5-Coder 32B: $0.00$, $p=.97$). With
Qwen2.5-Coder 32B on HumanEval+, the revised \auto{} stays within about two
points of Single-Gate at every level and exceeds it at $k=4$ (89.1\% against
88.5\%).
More samples per binding do not help when the samples fail for the same
reason, for example because the agent misreads the docstring in every sample.

\begin{answerbox}\textbf{The price in success.} \auto{} is not a way to raise task
success. In the main study, it succeeds less often than a single agent, because
weak builders often cannot write admissible teams and because, on class-level
tasks, even admitted and hand-written typed teams succeed less often than a
single agent; on function-level tasks, strong builders are on par with
multi-agent teams. A post-hoc revision recovers
much of the lost work on held-out tasks; with a weak model, mostly through a
hand-written fallback team, which also brings back silent failures. A larger budget does not close the gap: the pre-registered
hypotheses were not supported for either model.
\end{answerbox}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What \auto{} offers: a team whose results can be trusted and
explained} RQ1 and RQ2 support the two benefits, and RQ3 the mechanism behind
them: the typed format makes completion a property that the engine computes,
and the checker rejects teams whose goal is not covered by executed checks. The
Typed-NC ablation suggests that both parts contribute, and that the effect of
the checking loop differs between builders. Both benefits matter when
the cost of an undetected wrong result is high, for example when the output of
a team flows into a larger system without review, when several teams run
unattended, or when a failure must be explained to a person rather than merely
retried.

\paragraph{Why executing tests is not enough} One could expect any condition
that runs tests before declaring success to be as reliable as \auto{}.
Single-Gate refutes this: it executes the public examples under the same budget
cap, yet it fails silently more than twice as often as admitted \auto{} teams.
The difference is coverage.
Single-Gate stops when the examples it happens to run pass; \auto{}'s $\varphi$
is true only when every declared obligation has been checked, and two-sided
anchoring ensures that the checks and the producers both see the goal. The
detach mutants of RQ3 show why the second side matters: without it, a team can
pass its checks while its producers never saw the task's examples.

\paragraph{What \auto{} does not offer} \auto{} does not make agents reason
better, and it does not raise task success over a single agent; it trades
success for reliability. The trade-off is clearest for weak builders, whose
sessions are refused, and in the post-hoc revision, where recovering refused
sessions reintroduced silent failures for the weakest model. Its caution also
has a cost of its own: more than half of its correct ClassEval results are not
reported as done. We found no support for two further benefits that we had
hoped for: more budget did not help (Experiment 1), and we cannot say whether
\auto{} reduces composition-caused failures, because LLM coders did not agree
on failure causes (Section~\ref{sec:rqs}).

\paragraph{When to use \auto{}} The results suggest a simple rule. If the goal
is the highest success rate per token, a single agent with an execution gate
is the better choice. If the goal is a result that can be trusted without
re-checking, and failures that can be located and repaired, \auto{} with a
capable builder offers both, at 7--16 times the output tokens of a single agent. With a
weak builder, \auto{} remains honest, since refused sessions do not claim
success, but it does little useful work.

\paragraph{Builders and decomposition are the bottlenecks} Admission divides
the models sharply, and the most frequent first violation is a reference to a
feature that the builder never declared, an inconsistency within one proposal.
Because the checker's diagnostics are precise and cheap, they could serve as a
training signal for builders, which we have not explored. Builders also
imitate the worked examples (Section~\ref{sec:threats}), so the examples shape
the teams as much as the task does. Even admitted and
hand-written teams lose success on class-level tasks, which suggests that
per-method footprints omit context that a single agent uses; footprints that
include the class's shared state are a direct design option.

\paragraph{Implications} \emph{Researchers} evaluating multi-agent systems
should report silent failures and the precision of ``done'' alongside success,
and should include a compute-matched single agent with an execution gate as a
baseline. \emph{Framework builders} could run \W{2}, \W{4} and \W{5} as a lint
over LangGraph or CrewAI teams, whose agents, typed state and edges map onto
views, hand-offs and write rights; we have not evaluated such a lint.
\emph{Practitioners} who need trustworthy results should prefer completion
signals that are computed from executed checks covering the goal over signals
that agents emit.

\paragraph{Future work} The revised \auto{} was evaluated on two models only.
Running it on all seven models and both benchmarks, and finding a recovery
strategy for refused sessions that does not reintroduce silent failures, is the
most direct next step. A second is a repair that does not trade silent
failures for successes on class-level tasks, for example by accepting a
re-sampled upstream test only if it also passes the goal's examples. Others are
builders trained on the checker's diagnostics, validators whose adequacy is
itself checked, attribution replays that reproduce the runtime's feedback, and
a human-coded study of failure causes.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Construct validity} Each condition ends a run with its own signal,
so the reliability measures compare different mechanisms. This is intended: a
user sees exactly this signal. Silent and flagged failures improve when a
condition says done rarely, and refused sessions can never fail silently; we
therefore report precision, sensitivity and MCC with them, and repeat the
analysis on admitted sessions only. Hidden tests are an imperfect
oracle~\cite{liu2023evalplus}, and a run that passes them may still be wrong.
Injected faults (RQ2) and mutants (RQ3) are synthetic, and the injected fault
classes coincide with the classes that attribution distinguishes; we mitigated
this by injecting into real runs, by mutating teams that LLMs wrote, and by
having a different model seed defects from plain-language definitions, but the
attribution of natural failures remains untested. The seeded defects are
labelled by the seeder, and some of them are arguably not defects
(Section~\ref{sec:res-prev}). \code{test\_valid} recognises the examples in a test
syntactically, so a test that names an example without asserting it correctly
can be accepted.

\paragraph{Internal validity} The baselines are re-implementations of published
systems, and the runtime, checker, baselines and reference team come from the
same author. Typed conditions run on \tool{} and the others in a group-chat
harness with the same time-outs and sandbox. The Typed-NC ablation removes the
whole build--check--repair loop, not only the checker; our post-hoc splits of
its runs by admissibility and of \auto{}'s runs by repair round are
observational. Re-samples after a
rejection see the rejected value and the validator's message, whereas replays
in attribution do not, so a replay may succeed less often than the original
sampling and push a fault towards \textsc{Specification}, the class that
attribution assigns to about four in five natural faults. Stamps do not cover
sibling values, and a retry does not re-sample an accepted value that fails
$\mathit{valid}$; both can only cost success, not reliability, because such
runs never say done. The runtime does not
confine validators to their owner's declared tools, so \W{6} is a property of
the specification only. The revision of Section~\ref{sec:res-cost} was designed
after seeing the main results; we piloted it on a development subset,
evaluated it on the other tasks, did not change the baselines, and report it
separately. Fixes were made during the pilot and the main runs; each
invalidated run was redone, and both logs are in the package. One model (Gemma~3) replaced a planned model for a technical reason
before any of its results existed. LLM outputs are
non-deterministic~\cite{ouyang2025nondeterminism}; we use three seeds and
task-clustered intervals.

\paragraph{External validity} Results may depend on the three worked examples
given to builders, whose choice we did not vary: 71\% of the admitted ClassEval
teams (911 of 1{,}279) reuse the agents of one of the examples, although 207
distinct team shapes appear. We study only Python tasks with docstring examples, which favour
executable validators and which the lifter relies on, and open-weight models of
up to 32B parameters, run with 4-bit quantisation. Larger or unquantised models
may write admissible teams more often, which would reduce the price of \auto{}
but not necessarily change its reliability. The transcript-based attribution
methods were run with a 32B judge, not with the proprietary models of their
original papers.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity} We report task-clustered intervals and exact
paired tests, and Holm-correct the pairwise success comparisons. The success
comparisons use task-level majority outcomes over the three seeds, which
discards some information; the confidence intervals of RQ1 use all runs. The
reliability differences of RQ1 are large compared with their intervals, except
for precision and MCC on ClassEval, where \auto{} and Single cannot be
separated. Experiment~1 was pre-registered; its exploratory comparison of the
revised and the original \auto{} was not.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\auto{} asks an LLM builder for a typed team of views and hybrid M2M
transformations, run by \tool{}, and admits the team only if a program can check
that its parts fit. In 44{,}352 runs with seven open-weight models, \auto{} teams
had the fewest silent failures of all eight conditions and flagged more than
90\% of their failures. Part of this came from abstaining, but admitted teams
still failed silently about half as often as a single agent, and on
function-level tasks their ``done'' was the most reliable signal of success.
When they failed, their traces located the responsible binding of 96--98\% of
injected faults and named the fault class more than twice as often as
transcript-based methods, and checked repairs matched rebuilding at lower cost.
A check that takes milliseconds caught most independently seeded defects, which
LLM critics could not tell from clean teams. These benefits come at a price:
lower success, 7--16 times the output tokens of a single agent, many refused sessions
for weak builders, and correct results that are not reported as done. \auto{}
is therefore not a way to solve more tasks, but a way to know more reliably
which tasks were solved and why the others were not, following one principle:
LLMs propose; programs decide.
